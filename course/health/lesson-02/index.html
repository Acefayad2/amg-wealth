01

Ask what changes

Marcus thinks about buying a motorcycle, then decides against it. He has avoided that exposure entirely, although he has not avoided every risk in life. He adds a handrail to the icy steps. That reduces the chance of a fall; it does not make a fall impossible. He also keeps an emergency fund to pay small medical bills himself. That is retention: he keeps the financial responsibility. To classify any action, ask what changed. Did the exposure disappear, did the odds fall, or did Marcus simply decide to pay for the loss himself?

Avoidance

Do not take the exposure

Reduction

Lower the chance or the size

Retention

Keep the cost yourself

02

Share it or transfer it

Sharing spreads a burden among participants. A group of neighbors who agree to help each other with medical bills are sharing. Transfer moves a specified financial obligation to another party under a contract. When Marcus buys a medical plan, the insurer accepts the obligation to pay covered expenses. Most health plans mix the methods. A deductible and coinsurance are retention inside a transfer: Marcus keeps the first part of each loss and a percentage of the rest, and the insurer takes the balance. Recognizing that mixture explains most cost-sharing questions.

Deductible

Marcus retains the first dollars

Coinsurance

Marcus and the insurer share the middle

Insurer pays

Transfer of the rest

03

Why pooling works

An insurer cannot predict whether Marcus will need surgery this year. It can predict, within a narrow range, how many people out of one hundred thousand similar adults will. That is the law of large numbers: as the number of similar exposure units grows, actual results come closer to expected results. Pooling collects premiums from the many to pay the losses of the few. The insurer still faces the difference between expected and actual claims, so it sets premiums with a margin and manages how many similar risks it accepts.

100 lives

Results swing widely

10,000 lives

Results settle near the expected rate

Premium

Expected claims plus expenses and margin

04

Adverse selection

People who expect to need care are the most eager to buy coverage, and people who feel healthy are the most likely to skip it. That tendency is adverse selection. Left alone it raises average claims and pushes premiums up until only the sickest remain. Insurers answer with underwriting, waiting periods, group enrollment rules and, in the individual market, limited open enrollment windows. Federal law now limits how far a health insurer can go. It cannot refuse or price comprehensive medical coverage based on health status, which is one reason enrollment periods matter so much today.

Adverse selection

The likeliest claimants buy first

Controls

Underwriting, waiting periods, enrollment windows

Federal limit

No health-status pricing in comprehensive medical plans

05

What makes a risk insurable

Insurers look for a large number of similar exposure units, so the law of large numbers applies. The loss must be definite, meaning identifiable as to time, place and amount, and measurable in dollars. It must be accidental from the insured’s viewpoint, not planned. The loss should not be catastrophic to the pool, such as one event hitting every insured at once. The chance of loss must be calculable and the premium economically feasible. These are characteristics insurers prefer, not a guarantee that every risk meeting them will be accepted at standard rates.

Many similar units

Predictable averages

Definite and measurable

Time, place and dollars

Accidental, not catastrophic

Unplanned and spread out

06

The insurer transfers risk too

After accepting Marcus and thousands of others, the insurer may buy reinsurance. It cedes part of its risk to a reinsurer, which agrees to reimburse a share of large or accumulated claims. The insurer that issued the policy is the ceding company. Marcus is not a party to that agreement. His contract remains with the original insurer, and he files claims there. Reinsurance lets an insurer write more business, protect itself against a severe year and smooth its results. On a question, keep reinsurance in the insurer-to-insurer lane.

Policyholder

Ceding insurer

Owes the policy benefits

Reinsurer

Reimburses the insurer for a share

More detail

A deductible is retention, not avoidance

Choosing a higher deductible means keeping more of each loss. It lowers premium because the insured retains more risk. It does not avoid the exposure or reduce the chance that a loss occurs.

Example: Rosa raises her deductible from $1,000 to $3,000. She has retained $2,000 more of each covered loss in exchange for a lower premium.

Expected versus actual claims

The law of large numbers narrows the gap between expected and actual results but never closes it completely. Insurers hold capital and buy reinsurance to absorb the remaining difference.

Example: An insurer expects 500 hospital admissions among 50,000 members and sees 530. Pricing margin and reinsurance absorb the extra 30.
