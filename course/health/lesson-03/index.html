01

Who owns the company

Priya owns a bakery with twenty workers and is collecting quotes. The first carrier is a stock insurer, owned by shareholders who expect a profit; its policies are usually nonparticipating. The second is a mutual insurer, owned by its policyholders, who may receive dividends when results are good; those policies are participating. A fraternal benefit society sells only to its members through a lodge system and operates on a nonprofit basis. A large employer might instead self-insure, paying its own claims from company funds and hiring an administrator to process them, often with stop-loss protection behind it.

Stock insurer

Owned by shareholders; nonparticipating

Mutual insurer

Owned by policyholders; may pay dividends

Fraternal society

Members only; lodge system; nonprofit

Self-insurer

Employer funds its own claims

02

Private versus government

Private insurers sell voluntary coverage for a premium. Government programs fill gaps the private market leaves. Medicare covers people 65 and older and certain disabled people. Medicaid serves people with limited income. Social Security pays disability income after a strict test. Workers compensation covers job injuries under state law, and in Maryland employers buy that coverage from private insurers or the Chesapeake Employers’ Insurance Company. TRICARE serves military families. When a question mixes programs and policies, decide first whether the payer is a private company accepting premium or a government program funded by taxes and mandates.

Private

Voluntary, premium-funded policies

Government

Medicare, Medicaid, Social Security, workers compensation, TRICARE

03

Authorized, and from where

An admitted, or authorized, insurer holds a certificate of authority from the Maryland Insurance Administration and may sell in the state. A nonadmitted insurer does not, and its policies lack the protection of Maryland’s guaranty corporation. Separately, every insurer has a home. A domestic insurer is incorporated in Maryland. A foreign insurer is incorporated in another state, such as Delaware. An alien insurer is incorporated in another country. Foreign does not mean overseas on the exam. A Pennsylvania company selling in Baltimore is foreign and, if it holds a certificate, admitted.

Admitted

Holds a Maryland certificate of authority

Domestic

Incorporated in Maryland

Foreign

Incorporated in another state

Alien

Incorporated in another country

04

Reading the insurer’s health

Before Priya trusts a carrier with her staff’s claims, Theo checks its financial strength. Independent rating services such as A.M. Best, Standard and Poor’s, Moody’s and Fitch grade insurers on their ability to pay claims. Operating results, including loss ratios and surplus, show whether the company is pricing soundly. Ratings are opinions, not guarantees, and a producer must never misrepresent them. Maryland regulators also monitor solvency through required financial statements and examinations. A strong rating lowers the chance that the guaranty corporation ever has to step in.

Rating services

Independent opinion of claims-paying ability

Operating results

Loss ratio, surplus, reserves

Regulatory review

Financial statements and examinations

05

How coverage reaches the buyer

Insurers distribute coverage in several ways. A career or captive agency system uses producers who represent one insurer. An independent agency system uses producers who place business with several insurers and own their expirations. Brokers represent the buyer in placing coverage, although in Maryland both agents and brokers hold the same producer license. Direct response sells by mail, phone or internet with no producer. Managing general agents administer business for insurers in a territory. Banks and employers also serve as channels. The system affects who the producer represents and how commissions flow.

Captive system

One insurer, exclusive producers

Independent system

Several insurers, producer owns the book

Direct response

No producer; mail, phone, online

06

Maryland’s three provider types

Maryland law recognizes three kinds of organizations that deliver health benefits. An insurer, defined broadly in the Insurance Article, is a person engaged as a principal in the business of insurance. A nonprofit health service plan is organized under Title 14 to provide health services on a prepaid or indemnity basis to subscribers, not for profit. A health maintenance organization combines financing and delivery of care, is authorized under the Health-General Article and must hold a certificate of authority from the Insurance Commissioner, with the Maryland Department of Health overseeing quality. All three must obey Maryland’s mandated benefits and consumer protections.

Insurer

Principal in the business of insurance

Nonprofit health service plan

Title 14; subscribers; not for profit

HMO

Finances and delivers care; certificate of authority

More detail

Admitted status and guaranty protection

The Maryland Life and Health Insurance Guaranty Corporation covers policies of member insurers, which are the insurers and HMOs authorized to do business in Maryland. Coverage from a nonadmitted carrier generally falls outside that protection.

Example: A Maryland family buys a plan from an unauthorized out-of-state company that later fails. The guaranty corporation does not step in for that policy.

Subscribers versus insureds

People covered by a nonprofit health service plan or an HMO are called subscribers or members rather than insureds, and their document is a subscriber contract or evidence of coverage rather than a policy. The protections are similar; the vocabulary differs.

Example: Priya’s HMO quote refers to her workers as members and to copayments rather than coinsurance.
