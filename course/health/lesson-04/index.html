01

Whom does Theo represent?

Theo has just joined AMG and holds a Maryland producer license. Under agency law, the insurer is the principal and the producer is its agent. The producer acts on the insurer’s behalf, and the insurer is responsible for acts within the producer’s authority. A captive producer represents a single insurer. An independent producer represents several. Maryland uses one license, insurance producer, for people who historically were called agents or brokers. Whatever the label, when a producer sells an insurer’s policy, the insurer is the party whose authority is being exercised.

Insurer

Principal

Producer

Agent acting for the insurer

Applicant

Third party relying on the agent

02

Express authority

The agency contract between Theo’s agency and the insurer spells out what he may do: solicit applications, quote approved rates, collect initial premiums and deliver policies. That written grant is express authority. It may also list what he may not do, such as alter a policy or accept a risk the underwriters have not approved. A producer who acts within express authority binds the insurer. A producer who exceeds it may still bind the insurer in some cases, which is why the next two kinds of authority exist.

Written grant

Solicit, quote, collect, deliver

Written limits

No altering policies or binding unapproved risks

03

Implied authority

The contract cannot list every act. Implied authority covers what the public reasonably assumes a producer may do to carry out the express duties. If Theo may collect initial premiums, he impliedly may give a receipt and explain how coverage begins. If he may deliver a policy, he may answer ordinary questions about it. Implied authority comes from the position itself. It is still real authority, so the insurer is bound by it. On a question, if an act is a normal, necessary step toward an express task, call it implied.

Express task

Collect the premium

Implied step

Issue a receipt and explain the start of coverage

Result

Insurer is bound

04

Apparent authority

Apparent authority arises from what the insurer lets the public see. Suppose the insurer gives Theo business cards, application forms and a desk in an office with its logo, then quietly revokes his authority but tells no one. Rosa, an applicant, reasonably believes he still represents the company. Because the insurer created that appearance, it may be bound by what Theo does. Apparent authority protects innocent third parties; it never protects a producer who knows he has no authority. The key word is reasonable. The appearance must come from the insurer’s conduct, not the producer’s claims alone.

Created by the insurer

Logo, forms, office, past dealings

Relied on reasonably

Applicant believes authority exists

Effect

Insurer may be bound

05

Knowledge travels to the insurer

Because the producer is the insurer’s agent, what the producer knows the insurer is treated as knowing. If Rosa tells Theo about a back injury and he leaves it off the application, the insurer may not later claim it was never informed. The producer also owes duties to applicants and insureds. He must handle premiums as a fiduciary, describe coverage accurately, disclose what the policy does not do, submit applications promptly and deliver the policy. These duties come from license law and from the general law of agency, and breaking them can cost the license.

Imputed knowledge

What the agent knows, the insurer knows

Fiduciary handling

Premiums belong to the insurer, not the producer

Accurate disclosure

Say what the policy does and does not do

06

Sort the facts

Here is the method. Find the act. Ask whether a written contract allows it; that is express authority. Ask whether it is a normal step toward an allowed act; that is implied. Ask whether the insurer’s own conduct led a reasonable applicant to believe the act was authorized; that is apparent. Then ask who is harmed if the act is disowned. Agency law generally protects the applicant who relied in good faith and leaves the insurer to recover from a producer who overstepped. Keep the producer’s separate duties to the client in view as well.

Express

In the contract

Implied

Needed to do the job

Apparent

Created by the insurer’s conduct

Duty

Fiduciary and truthful

More detail

The producer is not the applicant’s agent for most purposes

Even when a producer shops several insurers for a client, the signed agency contracts run to the insurers, and premiums collected belong to them. Maryland imposes fiduciary duties on the producer precisely because the money and the authority are the insurer’s.

Example: Rosa pays Theo a first premium in cash. Theo must deposit it for the insurer promptly; using it for office rent breaches his fiduciary duty.

Apparent authority has a limit

An applicant who knows a producer lacks authority cannot claim apparent authority, and the appearance must trace back to the insurer. A producer’s own exaggerations do not bind an insurer that did nothing to create the impression.

Example: A former producer whose contract ended, and who returned all forms and materials, prints his own flyers claiming to represent the insurer. The insurer did not create that appearance.
