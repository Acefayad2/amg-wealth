01

Four elements, one application

Rosa, a self-employed electrician, applies for a medical plan. Her completed application and first premium are the offer. The insurer accepts by issuing the policy as applied for. If the insurer issues it with changes, that is a counteroffer she accepts by paying or keeping it. Consideration flows both ways: Rosa gives premium and truthful statements; the insurer gives its promise to pay covered expenses. Both parties must be competent, meaning of legal age and sound mind, and the contract must have a legal purpose. A contract missing any element is not enforceable.

Offer

Application plus premium

Acceptance

Insurer issues the policy

Consideration

Premium and statements for a promise to pay

Competent parties and legal purpose

Required throughout

02

Adhesion and aleatory

Rosa did not negotiate the wording. The insurer wrote the policy and she took it or left it. That makes it a contract of adhesion, and courts read genuine ambiguities against the drafter, the insurer. The contract is also aleatory: the values exchanged are unequal and depend on chance. Rosa might pay small premiums and collect a large hospital bill, or pay for years and collect nothing. Neither party is cheated by that imbalance, because both agreed to accept it. These two characteristics explain why insurance disputes are decided differently from disputes over an ordinary purchase.

Adhesion

Written by one side; ambiguity read against the insurer

Aleatory

Unequal exchange depending on chance

03

Personal, unilateral and conditional

The policy is personal. It covers Rosa, and she cannot hand it to a neighbor; the insurer agreed to insure her health, not anyone’s. It is unilateral. Only the insurer makes an enforceable promise. Rosa is not legally required to keep paying, but if she stops, the promise lapses. It is conditional. The insurer’s duty to pay depends on conditions being met, such as premiums paid and proof of loss submitted on time. When a question asks why an insured cannot be sued for nonpayment, the answer is that the contract is unilateral.

Personal

Follows the insured, not transferable

Unilateral

Only the insurer promises

Conditional

Pay premiums, give proof of loss

04

Reading the words

Because the insurer drafted the contract, ambiguous language is interpreted the way a reasonable insured would understand it. The reasonable expectations doctrine goes a step further in some courts: coverage the buyer reasonably expected may be honored even if fine print says otherwise. Two more principles shape health insurance. Indemnity means the policy restores a loss but should not create profit, which is why medical expense plans reimburse actual charges. Utmost good faith means both sides must deal honestly. Rosa relies on the insurer’s solvency and wording; the insurer relies on her answers.

Ambiguity

Read against the drafter

Reasonable expectations

What the buyer reasonably understood

Indemnity

Restore, do not profit

Utmost good faith

Both sides deal honestly

05

Representations, warranties, concealment and fraud

Rosa’s application answers are representations: statements she believes true. A material misrepresentation, one that would have changed the underwriting decision, lets the insurer rescind within the contestable period. A warranty is a statement guaranteed to be true, and in health insurance statements are treated as representations, not warranties, unless the policy says otherwise. Concealment is deliberately withholding a material fact the insurer did not ask about but should have been told. Fraud is intentional deception to gain a benefit. The difference is intent and materiality, and the remedies grow more severe along that line.

Representation

Believed true; material errors allow rescission

Warranty

Guaranteed true; rare in health policies

Concealment

Withholding a material fact

Fraud

Intentional deception

06

Waiver and estoppel

Suppose Rosa pays her premium twelve days late and the insurer accepts it without comment, then does so again. By voluntarily giving up its right to insist on prompt payment, the insurer has waived that right for those payments. Estoppel is the consequence: having led Rosa to rely on its conduct, the insurer is stopped from later denying a claim because a payment was late. Waiver is the voluntary surrender of a known right; estoppel prevents a party from reversing course after another relied on it. Together they keep insurers from using technicalities after their own conduct excused them.

Waiver

Voluntarily giving up a known right

Estoppel

Barred from reversing after reliance

More detail

Materiality decides rescission

Not every wrong answer lets an insurer rescind. The misstatement must be material: the insurer must show it would have declined or issued differently had it known the truth. Immaterial errors do not void coverage.

Example: Rosa lists her weight five pounds low. That is immaterial. Omitting a recent hospitalization is material.

Indemnity in disability income

Disability income policies pay a stated monthly benefit rather than reimbursing bills, so indemnity is enforced differently. Insurers limit benefits to a percentage of earnings and use a relation-of-earnings provision so a disabled person does not profit from being disabled.

Example: An electrician earning $6,000 a month is offered at most about $3,600 to $4,000 of monthly benefit.
