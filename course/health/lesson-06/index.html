Explanations & examples

01

Six needs in one household

Marcus and Elena list what could go wrong financially. Marcus could be too hurt to work, which is a loss of income. Anyone could face hospital and physician bills, which is medical expense. The children need cleanings and fillings, which is dental expense. Elena’s mother may someday need help with daily living, which is long-term care expense. Glasses and prescriptions are smaller, predictable costs. Each need has a matching benefit. Health insurance is a family of products, not one product, and the exam expects you to name the right member of the family for each loss.

Loss of income

Disability income insurance

Medical expense

Medical plans

Dental expense

Dental plans

Long-term care expense

Long-term care insurance

02

Vision, prescriptions and specified disease

Vision benefits usually cover an exam and an allowance toward lenses or frames on a schedule. Prescription drug benefits may be built into a medical plan or sold separately, with copayments that differ by drug tier. Specified disease, or dread disease, policies pay only if a named illness such as cancer is diagnosed, often as a lump sum or scheduled amount regardless of other coverage. They are supplements, not substitutes for medical insurance. A producer who presents a cancer policy as full health coverage misleads the buyer and may violate Maryland’s advertising rules.

Vision

Exam and allowance on a schedule

Prescription

Copayments by drug tier

Specified disease

Pays only for the named illness

03

Accidental death and dismemberment

AD and D pays a principal sum if an accident causes death, usually within a stated period after the injury. It pays a capital sum, often half the principal sum, for loss of a limb or sight in one eye, and typically the full principal sum for two such losses. It pays nothing for death from sickness. AD and D is sold alone, as a rider, or as part of group plans and travel coverage. Because the benefit is a fixed amount rather than reimbursement of bills, it is a valued benefit, and the accident definition in Lesson 1 decides whether it responds.

Principal sum

$100,000 for accidental death

Capital sum

$50,000 for one limb or one eye

Two losses

Usually the full principal sum

04

Individual or group

Rosa buys her own medical plan. The insurer underwrites her, she owns the policy and she pays the whole premium. That is individual coverage. Marcus is covered at work. His employer holds the master policy, he receives a certificate of coverage, the group is underwritten as a whole and the employer often pays part of the premium. That is group coverage. Group plans spread risk across the whole workforce, cost less per person to administer and have special rules about eligibility, continuation and conversion that we will study later. Individual plans follow the person wherever she goes.

Individual

Owns the policy; underwritten alone; portable

Group

Master policy and certificates; employer may contribute

05

Private or government

Rosa’s and Marcus’s plans are private: a company accepts premium and promises benefits under a contract. Dennis, who turns 65 next year, will join Medicare, a federal program funded by payroll taxes and premiums. Gloria, his mother, might someday qualify for Medicaid if her savings run out. Social Security disability and workers compensation are also government programs. Private coverage is voluntary and contractual; government coverage is created by statute and open to people who meet the statutory tests. Many families hold both, and later lessons explain which pays first.

Private

Contract for premium: Rosa, Marcus

Government

Statutory programs: Medicare, Medicaid, Social Security, workers compensation

06

Limited or comprehensive

A comprehensive medical plan covers a wide range of hospital, surgical, physician and preventive services, subject to deductibles and coinsurance. A limited policy covers only a narrow peril or pays a small fixed amount: accident-only, hospital indemnity, specified disease, dental-only or vision-only. Limited policies are legitimate supplements, but Maryland requires them to disclose their limited nature so no one mistakes a $200-a-day hospital policy for health insurance. When you classify a policy, use all three axes: individual or group, private or government, limited or comprehensive.

Individual or group

Axis 2

Private or government

Axis 3

Limited or comprehensive

More detail

Valued versus reimbursement benefits

Medical expense plans reimburse or pay providers for actual charges, honoring indemnity. AD&D, hospital indemnity, specified disease and disability income pay stated amounts regardless of bills. Recognizing which type a policy is explains how it coordinates with other coverage.

Example: A hospital indemnity policy pays $200 a day for ten days even though the medical plan paid the entire hospital bill.

Long-term care is not medical expense

Long-term care insurance pays for custodial and personal care, such as help with bathing and dressing, that medical plans and Medicare generally exclude. It is a distinct product with its own Maryland regulation.

Example: Gloria’s medical plan pays for a hospital stay after a fall but not for a year of help at home afterward; a long-term care policy would.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Identify the principal types of health losses and the benefits that respond to them.
  • Describe accidental death and dismemberment and specified disease benefits.
  • Classify a policy as individual or group, private or government, limited or comprehensive.
