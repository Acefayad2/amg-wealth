01

What makes a policy limited

Elena’s coworker shows her a brochure: two hundred dollars a day while in the hospital, for forty dollars a month. Is that health insurance? No. It is a limited policy. Limited policies restrict either the perils they cover, such as accidents only, or the amounts they pay, such as a fixed daily sum unrelated to the bill. They are useful supplements and poor substitutes. Because buyers so often mistake them for comprehensive coverage, the policy must carry a prominent notice, typically on the first page, stating that it is a limited policy and describing what it does not cover.

Limited perils

Accident only; one disease

Limited amounts

Fixed daily or lump sum

Required notice

“This is a limited policy” on the first page

02

Accident-only and specified disease

An accident-only policy pays for injuries from accidents and nothing for sickness. It may pay medical expenses, a disability income benefit, or AD and D sums, but the trigger is always an accident. A specified disease, or dread disease, policy pays only on diagnosis of the named illness, most often cancer, and usually pays scheduled or lump-sum amounts regardless of what other coverage pays. Rosa might buy a cancer policy to cover travel and lost income during treatment. She must still carry a medical plan for everything else, and Theo must say so plainly.

Accident-only

Injuries only; no sickness benefits

Specified disease

Named illness only; pays regardless of other coverage

03

Hospital indemnity and credit disability

A hospital indemnity policy pays a fixed amount for each day the insured is confined, regardless of the actual charges, which is why it is sometimes called hospital income. It helps with deductibles and lost pay but does not cover physician or outpatient bills. Credit disability insurance pays the installments on a specific loan while the borrower is disabled. The creditor is the beneficiary, the benefit cannot exceed the debt, and the premium is often added to the loan. When Dennis finances a car, he may be offered credit disability; he may decline it without losing the loan.

Hospital indemnity

Fixed daily amount; ignores the bill

Credit disability

Pays loan installments; creditor is beneficiary

04

Blanket insurance

Priya sponsors a softball team and buys blanket accident coverage. Blanket insurance covers a changing group of people exposed to a common hazard without naming each one: a team’s players, passengers on a bus, students at a school, campers at a summer camp. No individual certificates are issued because the members change. Benefits usually pay medical expenses from accidents during the covered activity. Blanket coverage is group coverage in form, but it insures a category of people rather than enrolled individuals, and that difference is the point the exam tests.

Covered

A class of people: team, passengers, students

No certificates

Members change constantly

Trigger

Accidents during the covered activity

05

Prescription, vision and critical illness

Separate vision plans cover eye care, and drug plans cover the prescriptions they list. A critical illness policy pays a lump sum for a listed diagnosis after any required survival period. You can use that money for rent, travel or another expense. Some limited benefits qualify for an exception to federal coverage rules. Separate limited vision benefits can qualify. So can independent critical illness coverage that does not coordinate with another plan. The exam term is excepted benefits: E, as in exception. A drug policy does not qualify just because it covers only prescriptions.

Prescription and vision

Scheduled benefits, nothing else

Critical illness

Lump sum on diagnosis of a listed condition

Excepted benefits

Certain federal requirements do not apply when the coverage meets the conditions

06

Short-term medical in Maryland

Short-term limited duration policies bridge gaps, such as the weeks between jobs. They are medically underwritten, exclude pre-existing conditions and need not include essential health benefits. Federal rules allow longer terms, but Maryland is stricter: a short-term policy sold in Maryland may last less than three months and may not be renewed or extended. Theo’s client who leaves a job in May should compare a short-term plan with a special enrollment on Maryland Health Connection or Maryland continuation coverage, which provide comprehensive benefits. Short-term coverage is a stopgap, and the notice must say so.

Term in Maryland

Less than 3 months

Renewal

Not permitted

Benefits

Underwritten; pre-existing conditions excluded

More detail

Limited policies do not coordinate

Most limited policies pay their stated benefit regardless of other insurance, because they are valued rather than reimbursement contracts. That is their appeal and their risk: a buyer may hold several and still face uncovered medical bills.

Example: A hospital indemnity policy pays $200 a day while the medical plan pays the hospital; the indemnity money is the insured’s to keep.

Credit disability amounts are tied to the debt

Benefits under credit disability may not exceed the loan payment or balance, and coverage ends when the loan is repaid. The creditor cannot require the borrower to buy it from a particular producer.

Example: Dennis’s $450 monthly car payment is the most the credit disability policy pays each month he is disabled.
