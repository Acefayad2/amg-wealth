01

The first underwriter

Before Rosa’s application reaches the home office, Theo has already underwritten it in her kitchen. Field underwriting means the producer’s screening of the applicant: observing her health and occupation, asking every question on the form, recording answers exactly as given and noting anything the insurer should know in the producer’s report. Good field underwriting saves the insurer from issuing policies it would have declined and saves applicants from policies that would be rescinded later for misstatements. It also protects the pool from adverse selection. The producer who skips this role invites claim disputes and license complaints.

02

Completing the application

The application must be complete and accurate. Theo reads each question aloud and writes Rosa’s answer; he never guesses or leaves a blank, because the insurer may treat an unanswered question as waived. If an answer is corrected, Rosa initials the change. She signs the application, and so does Theo as the witnessing producer. If Rosa pays the first premium now, Theo gives a conditional receipt, which makes coverage effective as of the application or exam date if the insurer later approves her as applied for. If she does not pay now, no coverage exists until delivery.

03

Requirements at delivery

When the policy arrives, Theo delivers it promptly and in person when he can. If the first premium was not paid with the application, he collects it and obtains a statement of continued good health, in which Rosa confirms her health has not changed since she applied. The policy is not in force until that premium is paid. Theo reviews the policy against the application, explains the benefits, exclusions, renewability and the free-look period, and delivers any required outline of coverage and the guaranty corporation notice. He has Rosa sign a delivery receipt, which fixes the date the free-look period begins.

04

Errors and omissions

Even careful producers make mistakes: a benefit amount misquoted, a replacement handled badly, an application mailed late so a client was uninsured when a claim arose. Errors and omissions insurance is professional liability coverage that pays defense costs and damages when a client sues over the producer’s negligent act or omission. It does not cover fraud, intentional misconduct or fines imposed by the regulator. Agencies carry it for the firm and its producers. The best E and O protection is process: documented recommendations, signed applications, delivery receipts and notes of what the client was told.

Covered

Negligent errors and omissions in professional service

Not covered

Fraud, intentional acts, regulatory fines

05

Clients approaching Medicare

Dennis, a client with an individual medical plan, turns 65 in March. Theo has a responsibility to tell him what that means. Medicare eligibility changes how his coverage coordinates, opens a seven-month initial enrollment window around his birthday, and starts the six-month Medicare supplement open enrollment when he enrolls in Part B. Group plans and Maryland continuation coverage end or change when Medicare entitlement begins, and late enrollment in Part B or Part D can bring permanent premium penalties. Notifying clients of Medicare eligibility in time is part of serving them, and insurers include similar notices in group certificates.

06

From kitchen table to in-force policy

Here is the sequence. Theo screens Rosa honestly and records her answers exactly. She signs; he gives a conditional receipt if she pays now. The home office underwrites using the sources from Lesson 12 and issues the policy, perhaps with changes. Theo delivers it, collects any premium due, gets a statement of continued good health if needed, explains the contract and starts the free-look clock with a signed receipt. He keeps records in case a question arises later, and he calendars Dennis’s 65th birthday. Each step has a purpose: an enforceable, understood contract and a protected client.

More detail

Conditional receipt versus binding receipt

Most health insurers use conditional receipts: coverage is effective as of the application or medical exam date only if the applicant is found insurable as applied for. A binding receipt, which provides coverage from the application date regardless of insurability until the insurer acts, is uncommon in health insurance.

Example: Rosa pays with her application on June 1 and is approved standard on June 20. Under a conditional receipt, coverage dates from June 1.

Changes to an application

If the insurer issues the policy on different terms than applied for, the applicant must accept the counteroffer, usually by signing an amendment at delivery. A producer may never alter an application after the applicant signs it.

Example: The insurer adds a back exclusion to Rosa’s disability policy; she signs the amendment at delivery to accept it.
