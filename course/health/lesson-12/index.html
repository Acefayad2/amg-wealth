Explanations & examples

01

What the underwriter is deciding

Rosa’s disability income application reaches the home office. The underwriter must decide whether to accept her, at what price and with what conditions. The criteria are the applicant’s health history, age, occupation, income, hobbies, habits such as tobacco use and, for comprehensive medical coverage, the limits federal law now places on health-based decisions. Disability, long-term care and limited policies may still be fully underwritten. The underwriter is matching Rosa to a class of similar risks so the price reflects the expected claims for that class. Fair classification protects the pool; unfair discrimination is prohibited.

02

The application and the producer’s report

The application is the primary source. Part one collects identity, occupation and the coverage requested; part two asks medical questions. Rosa signs it, certifying her answers are true to the best of her knowledge, and it becomes part of the contract when attached to the policy. Theo adds a producer’s report, sometimes called an agent’s statement, giving his own observations: how long he has known her, whether the coverage replaces another policy, and anything he noticed. The producer’s report is not shown to the applicant, and it must be honest. It is the insurer’s first check on the application.

03

Physician statements and inspection reports

If Rosa’s answers mention a back injury, the underwriter may request an attending physician statement from the doctor who treated her, with her written authorization. The APS gives diagnosis, treatment and prognosis in the physician’s own words. The underwriter may also order an investigative consumer report, often called an inspection report, from an outside agency. It covers lifestyle, finances and reputation, drawn from records and interviews. The Fair Credit Reporting Act governs that report: Rosa must be told it may be ordered, may learn its nature and scope, and must be told if it leads to adverse action.

Attending physician statement

Medical detail from the treating doctor; needs authorization

Inspection report

Lifestyle and finances; FCRA notices apply

04

The Medical Information Bureau

The MIB is a membership organization of insurers that keeps coded summaries of medical and some nonmedical conditions reported by member companies when a person applied for individual life, health, disability or long-term care coverage. It exists to detect omissions and fraud across companies. The codes flag areas to investigate; an insurer may not decline solely because of an MIB code and must confirm the underlying facts. Applicants receive a notice that the MIB may be consulted, and they may request their own file and correct errors. MIB codes are not shared with employers or the public.

What it holds

Coded conditions from prior applications

What it is for

Catching omissions across insurers

Limits

No decline on a code alone; applicant may see and correct the file

05

Examinations, lab tests and HIV consent

For larger disability benefits or older applicants the insurer may require a paramedical exam, blood and urine tests, or a physician’s examination, at the insurer’s expense. Tests screen for conditions such as diabetes, liver disease and nicotine use. Testing for HIV requires the applicant’s specific informed consent on a separate form that explains the test, how results are kept confidential and to whom they may be disclosed. Maryland law treats HIV results as confidential medical information. An insurer may underwrite on a positive result but may not test without consent or reveal results improperly.

Exam or lab test

Ordered and paid by the insurer

HIV test

Separate written informed consent

Results

Confidential; used only for underwriting

06

Putting the file together

The underwriter now has Rosa’s application, Theo’s report, a physician statement about her back, an inspection report, an MIB check and lab results. Each source answers a different question, and each carries its own consent or notice requirement. The application and medical sources describe health. The inspection report describes lifestyle and finances. The MIB tests consistency. None of them is permitted to be used for purposes outside underwriting the coverage applied for. Lesson 13 shows how the underwriter turns this file into a risk class and where the law forbids certain distinctions.

Authorization travels with the application

Applicants sign a HIPAA-compliant authorization allowing the insurer to obtain medical records and to report to the MIB. Without it the insurer cannot request an APS. The authorization states its purpose and expiration.

Example: Rosa’s authorization lets the insurer contact her orthopedist about the back injury but not her employer about her job performance.

Who pays for underwriting information

The insurer pays for examinations, lab tests, physician statements, inspection reports and MIB inquiries. The applicant’s cost is time and disclosure, never a fee for being underwritten.

Example: The paramedical examiner visits Rosa at home at the insurer’s expense.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Describe the criteria an underwriter applies to an individual health application.
  • Identify each source of underwriting information and what it contributes.
  • Explain the consent and disclosure rules around medical examinations, lab tests and HIV testing.
