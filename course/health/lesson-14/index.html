Explanations & examples

01

The cheaper policy

Rosa receives a mailer offering a disability income policy for thirty dollars less a month than the one Theo sold her two years ago. Replacement means dropping or reducing existing coverage to buy new coverage. It is lawful and sometimes wise, but it resets clocks. A new disability or long-term care policy brings a new pre-existing condition look-back, a new probationary period and a new contestable period. Rosa’s back condition, now covered after two years, could become a pre-existing condition under the new policy. The first replacement question is always: what coverage has the client already earned?

Existing policy

Pre-existing exclusion satisfied; contestable period passed

Replacement

New look-back, new probationary period, new contestability

Risk

A covered condition becomes excluded again

02

Compare the whole contract

Price is one line in a comparison. Theo lays the two policies side by side. Does the new policy define disability as own occupation or any occupation? How long is the elimination period and the benefit period? Does it include a residual benefit, a cost-of-living rider, a future increase option? Is it noncancelable, guaranteed renewable or conditionally renewable? What does it exclude that the old policy covers? For medical plans, is the current plan grandfathered with features a new plan would lose? Only after the benefits, limitations and exclusions are compared can anyone say whether thirty dollars is a saving.

03

Underwriting cuts both ways

The new insurer will underwrite Rosa as she is today, not as she was two years ago. Her back condition could produce a higher rate, an exclusion rider or a declination. The cardinal rule of replacement is that the existing policy is never canceled until the new policy is issued, delivered and accepted on terms the client understands. A client who cancels first and is then declined has lost coverage she cannot get back. Theo also checks whether the old policy’s premium is locked by a noncancelable clause, a guarantee the new policy may not match.

Apply

New insurer underwrites current health

Receive

Policy issued, delivered, accepted

Then cancel

Only after the new coverage is in force

04

Medicare supplement and long-term care replacement

Two products carry specific Maryland replacement rules. When a Medicare supplement policy replaces another, the new insurer must credit the time already served and may not impose new pre-existing condition, waiting, elimination or probationary periods for benefits the old policy covered. Long-term care replacement works the same way, with a replacement notice and a comparison of benefits. Both require the producer to ask about existing coverage and to document the answer. Selling Gloria a second Medigap policy without replacing the first is prohibited duplication. The producer’s job is to make replacement a deliberate, documented decision.

Medigap replacement

Credit for time served; no new waiting periods for covered benefits

LTC replacement

Replacement notice, comparison, suitability review

05

When replacement makes sense

Replacement is right when the new policy fixes a real gap: a short benefit period, an any-occupation definition, a conditionally renewable clause, or a premium the client can no longer afford at all. It is right when the client’s health still qualifies for standard rates and the new policy’s waiting periods will not leave a known condition uncovered. It is wrong when the only gain is a small premium saving that the client pays for with lost guarantees or a newly excluded condition. Theo writes down the reasons either way; a documented recommendation protects the client and the producer.

Replace

Fixes a real gap; health still qualifies; no coverage lost

Keep

Small saving; lost guarantees; condition would be excluded

06

The replacement checklist

Before recommending any health insurance replacement, work the list. One: identify the coverage the client has already earned, including satisfied pre-existing and probationary periods. Two: compare definitions, periods, riders, renewability and exclusions, not just premium. Three: confirm the client’s current insurability and get the new policy issued and accepted before anything is canceled. Four: apply Medigap and long-term care replacement rules where they exist. Five: document the comparison and the client’s decision. A replacement that survives the checklist serves the client; one that does not is a sale, not advice.

More detail

Grandfathered plans and replacement

A medical plan in existence before the Affordable Care Act and kept without major changes is grandfathered and exempt from some requirements. Replacing it with a new plan ends grandfathered status permanently; the new plan must meet all ACA rules, which is usually a gain in benefits but may change networks and cost sharing.

Example: Marcus’s employer considers replacing a grandfathered plan; the new plan must cover preventive care without cost sharing but may use a narrower network.

Twisting is replacement by misrepresentation

Inducing a replacement through false or misleading comparisons is the unfair trade practice of twisting, covered in Lesson 54. Honest replacement is lawful; twisting is not, and the difference lies entirely in the accuracy of what the producer says.

Example: Telling Rosa her current insurer is about to fail, without basis, in order to sell a replacement is twisting.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Identify the pre-existing condition and waiting period risks created by replacement.
  • Compare benefits, limitations and exclusions between an existing and a proposed policy.
  • Sequence underwriting and cancellation so the client is never uninsured.
