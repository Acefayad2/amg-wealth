01

Why the provisions are uniform

Every state, including Maryland, adopted a model law listing provisions that individual health policies must contain and others they may contain. The required provisions protect the insured and may be worded differently only if the insurer’s version is at least as favorable. Rosa’s disability policy and Marcus’s individual medical plan therefore share the same skeleton. This lesson covers the provisions about the contract and the premium; Lesson 16 covers the claim clocks; Lesson 17 covers the optional provisions and renewability. The exam tests these as day counts and rules, so learn each number with its purpose.

Must appear; may only be made more favorable

Uniform

02

Entire contract and changes

The entire contract provision states that the policy, including any endorsements and the attached application, is the whole agreement. Nothing said outside those documents is part of the contract. No statement by the applicant can be used against her unless it is in the attached application. Changes to the policy are valid only if approved by an executive officer of the insurer and endorsed on or attached to the policy. A producer cannot change the policy or waive any of its provisions. If Theo promises Rosa a benefit the policy does not contain, the promise is not part of the contract, and he has exposed himself to liability.

03

Time limit on certain defenses

After the policy has been in force for two years, the insurer may no longer use misstatements in the application, other than fraudulent ones, to void the policy or deny a claim. It also may not deny a claim after those two years by calling a condition pre-existing unless the policy excluded it by name. This is the health insurance counterpart to the life insurance incontestability clause. Rosa’s innocent error about a past prescription cannot be raised against her in year three. Deliberate fraud remains contestable, and some policies use the stricter incontestable wording that drops even the fraud exception.

Years 1 to 2

Material misstatements may void or deny

After 2 years

Only fraudulent misstatements; no unnamed pre-existing denials

04

Grace period

A grace period gives the insured time to pay a late premium while coverage continues. The uniform provision requires at least seven days for weekly premium policies, ten days for monthly premium policies and thirty-one days for all other modes, such as quarterly or annual. A claim during the grace period is paid, although the insurer may deduct the overdue premium. If the premium is still unpaid when the grace period ends, the policy lapses as of the due date. Marcus pays monthly, so a premium due on the first is late on the eleventh, and his policy lapses if nothing arrives by then.

05

Reinstatement

A lapsed policy can come back. If the insurer accepts a late premium without requiring a reinstatement application, the policy is reinstated when the premium is accepted. If the insurer requires an application and issues a conditional receipt for the premium, the policy is reinstated when the insurer approves the application, or automatically on the forty-fifth day after the receipt date if the insurer has not disapproved it in writing. A reinstated policy covers accidents immediately but covers sickness only if it begins more than ten days after reinstatement. The insurer and insured otherwise have the same rights they had before the lapse.

Application required

Reinstated on approval or the 45th day without written disapproval

06

A premium goes unpaid

Follow Marcus. His monthly premium is due June 1 and he forgets. Through June 10 he is in the grace period; a June 7 injury is covered, less the overdue premium. On June 11 the policy lapses back to June 1. On July 20 he sends the premium with a reinstatement application and gets a conditional receipt. The insurer says nothing. On the forty-fifth day after July 20 the policy is reinstated automatically. An accident the next morning is covered; a sickness that begins three days later is not, because sickness must start more than ten days after reinstatement. Every step is a number from this lesson.

More detail

Required versus optional, favorable versus not

An insurer may substitute wording for a required provision only if the substitute is not less favorable to the insured. It may omit any optional provision entirely. When a policy and the law conflict, the conformity-with-statutes provision in Lesson 17 brings the policy into line.

Example: An insurer offering a 60-day grace period on monthly premiums has made the provision more favorable than the 10-day minimum, which is permitted.

Fraud survives the two-year limit

The standard time-limit-on-certain-defenses wording preserves the insurer’s right to contest fraudulent misstatements at any time. Carriers that use the incontestable alternative give up even that right after two years, which is a selling point the producer may describe accurately.

Example: A policy marked incontestable after two years cannot be voided in year four even for a knowingly false answer; a standard policy could be.
