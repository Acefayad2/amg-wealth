01

Notice of claim: 20 days

Marcus breaks his wrist on March 1. The notice of claim provision requires him to tell the insurer within 20 days of the loss, or as soon as reasonably possible. Notice can be a phone call or a letter to the insurer or its authorized producer, and it need only identify the insured and the policy. Late notice does not defeat the claim if it was given as soon as reasonably possible. For disability income policies that pay for a long period, the insured may be required to give proof of continuing disability at reasonable intervals, usually every six months.

02

Claim forms: 15 days

Once the insurer receives notice, it must send Marcus the forms it wants used for proof of loss within 15 days. If the forms do not arrive in that time, Marcus may submit proof in any written form describing the occurrence, character and extent of the loss, and the insurer must accept it. The provision prevents an insurer from stalling a claim by withholding paperwork. In practice the clock runs from the insurer’s receipt of notice, and producers often help clients by supplying the forms directly.

03

Proof of loss: 90 days

Written proof of loss must be furnished within 90 days after the loss. For a continuing loss such as disability, the 90 days run from the end of each period for which the insurer is liable. If it was not reasonably possible to meet the deadline, proof is accepted as soon as reasonably possible, but no later than one year after it was due, unless the claimant was legally incapacitated. Proof of loss is the medical and financial documentation that lets the insurer decide the claim. It is different from notice, which merely alerts the insurer that a claim is coming.

04

Time and manner of payment

After receiving proof, the insurer must pay claims immediately, which Maryland’s prompt payment law defines as within 30 days for clean claims, with interest at 1.5 percent a month starting on day 31. Benefits for a continuing loss, such as disability income, must be paid at least monthly. Death benefits go to the named beneficiary or, if none, to the estate. All other benefits go to the insured, or at the insured’s direction to the hospital or physician under an assignment of benefits. A facility of payment clause lets the insurer pay up to a small amount, often 1,000 dollars, to a relative who is caring for the insured.

05

Examination, autopsy and legal action

The insurer may have the insured examined by a physician of its choosing, at its own expense, as often as reasonably necessary while a claim is pending. In case of death it may require an autopsy where the law does not forbid one. The legal actions provision sets the window for suing the insurer: no suit may be filed until 60 days after proof of loss has been given, which gives the insurer time to pay, and no suit may be filed more than three years after proof of loss was required. Inside that window the insured keeps full access to the courts.

06

Beneficiaries and cancellation by the insured

Where a policy pays a death benefit, such as AD and D, the insured may change the beneficiary at any time unless the designation was made irrevocable; the consent of a revocable beneficiary is not needed. Maryland also lists cancellation by the insured among the provisions to know: an insured may cancel an individual policy by written notice, and the insurer returns the unearned premium. Put the clocks together for Marcus: notice within 20 days, forms within 15, proof within 90, payment within 30, suit between 60 days and three years. Say them in order until they are automatic.

Prompt payment in Maryland

Maryland’s prompt payment statute requires insurers, nonprofit health service plans and HMOs to pay or send a status notice within 30 days of receiving a claim. Late payments on clean claims carry interest of 1.5 percent a month for days 31 to 60, 2 percent for days 61 to 120 and 2.5 percent after that, added automatically.

Example: A hospital’s clean claim paid on day 50 includes 1.5 percent interest for the late period without the hospital asking.

Assignment of benefits

Although health policies are personal contracts, the insured may direct that medical benefits be paid to the provider. This assignment moves the payment, not the coverage, and is routine in hospital and physician billing.

Example: Marcus signs an assignment at the emergency room; the insurer pays the hospital directly and sends Marcus an explanation of benefits.
