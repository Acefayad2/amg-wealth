Explanations & examples

01

Fee-for-service or prepaid

Marcus has two quotes on his desk. The first is a traditional plan: he sees a doctor, the doctor bills for the service, and the plan reimburses a share of the charge. That is fee-for-service, also called indemnity: payment follows each service rendered. The second is a health maintenance organization: he pays a fixed monthly amount and the organization provides care through its own doctors and facilities. That is a prepaid basis: the money is paid in advance for care, not after each service. Almost every other difference between plans grows out of this one.

Fee-for-service

Pay per service after care; reimbursement

Prepaid

Fixed payment in advance; care delivered by the plan

02

Specified coverages or comprehensive care

Older basic plans listed specified coverages: a hospital expense benefit for room and board, a surgical expense benefit paying by a schedule, a physician expense benefit for office visits. Each had its own limits and gaps between them. Comprehensive care, the modern major medical approach, covers the full range of hospital, surgical, physician, diagnostic and preventive services under one deductible and coinsurance structure. The Affordable Care Act requires individual and small group plans to cover ten essential health benefit categories, which pushed the market firmly toward comprehensive design.

Specified coverages

Separate hospital, surgical, physician benefits with gaps

Comprehensive care

One structure covering the full range

03

Benefit schedule or usual, customary and reasonable

How much does the plan pay for an appendectomy? A benefit schedule names a flat dollar amount for each procedure, regardless of what the surgeon charges; the patient pays any excess. A usual, customary and reasonable approach pays based on the going rate: the fee the provider usually charges, what similar providers in the area customarily charge, and what is reasonable for the circumstances. Charges above UCR are the patient’s responsibility unless the provider is in network and has agreed to accept the plan’s allowance as payment in full.

Benefit schedule

Flat amount per procedure

Usual, customary, reasonable

Based on prevailing area charges

In-network

Provider accepts the allowance

04

Any provider or limited choice

An indemnity plan lets Marcus see any licensed provider and pays the same share wherever he goes. A managed care plan limits choice to a network of providers who have agreed to discounted fees and plan rules. An HMO generally pays nothing outside the network except emergencies. A PPO pays more in network and less out. The trade-off is simple: limited choice buys lower premiums and negotiated prices; any-provider freedom costs more. The exam describes a plan’s rules and asks you to name it, so anchor each name to its provider rule.

Any provider

Same benefits anywhere; higher cost

Limited choice

Network rules; negotiated prices; lower cost

05

Insureds, subscribers and participants

Vocabulary signals plan type. An insurance company issues a policy to an insured. A nonprofit health service plan or an HMO issues a subscriber contract or evidence of coverage to a subscriber or member. An employer’s self-funded plan covers participants under a plan document governed by ERISA. The words matter because the regulator, the guaranty protection and the contract documents differ. When Priya’s HMO quote calls her workers members and lists copayments instead of coinsurance, those words tell you it is a prepaid, limited-choice plan before you read the benefits.

Insured

Policy from an insurer

Subscriber or member

Contract from a service plan or HMO

Participant

Self-funded ERISA plan

06

Use the five pairs

Describe any plan with the five pairs. Marcus’s indemnity quote: fee-for-service, comprehensive, UCR reimbursement, any provider, insured. The HMO quote: prepaid, comprehensive, fixed copayments instead of UCR, limited choice with a gatekeeper, member. A PPO sits between them: fee-for-service with negotiated network rates, comprehensive, in-network allowance accepted as payment in full, two levels of provider choice, insured. Lessons 19 through 22 fill in the deductibles, coinsurance, gatekeepers and networks. The concepts here are the grammar; the plan names are the vocabulary.

Payment

Fee-for-service or prepaid

Scope

Specified or comprehensive

Amount

Schedule or UCR

Choice

Any provider or network

More detail

Balance billing

When a provider outside a network charges more than the plan’s allowed amount, the patient may be billed the difference. Federal law now protects patients from most surprise balance bills for emergency care and for out-of-network providers at in-network facilities.

Example: An out-of-network anesthesiologist at an in-network hospital may not balance bill Marcus beyond his in-network cost sharing.

Why prepaid plans emphasize prevention

A prepaid plan keeps the fixed payment whether or not members use services, so it profits by keeping members healthy. Fee-for-service plans pay more when more care is delivered. This difference explains why HMOs pioneered preventive care coverage.

Example: An HMO covers an annual physical with no copayment because preventing illness lowers its costs.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Contrast fee-for-service with prepaid plans.
  • Contrast specified coverages with comprehensive care and benefit schedules with usual, customary and reasonable charges.
  • Contrast any-provider plans with limited-choice networks and insureds with subscribers.
