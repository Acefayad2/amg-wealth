01

What major medical is

Major medical insurance is the comprehensive plan most people mean by health insurance. It covers a broad range of hospital, surgical, physician, diagnostic, prescription and preventive services, with high or unlimited maximums, in exchange for cost sharing by the insured. Historically it was sold as a supplement over a basic hospital plan or as a comprehensive plan standing alone; today the comprehensive form dominates. Federal law forbids lifetime and annual dollar limits on essential health benefits and caps the insured’s annual out-of-pocket spending. Common exclusions remain: cosmetic surgery, experimental treatment, custodial care, and adult dental and vision.

Broad coverage

Hospital, surgical, physician, drugs, preventive

Cost sharing

Deductible, coinsurance, copayments

Limits

No dollar maximums on essential benefits; usual exclusions remain

02

Kinds of deductibles

A deductible is the amount the insured pays before the plan pays. Most are calendar-year deductibles that reset each January. A per-cause deductible applies to each separate illness or injury instead. Family plans use an individual deductible for each person and a family deductible, often two or three times the individual amount, after which everyone is covered. A carryover provision lets expenses in the last three months of a year count toward next year’s deductible. A common accident provision applies one deductible when several family members are hurt in the same accident. Preventive services are covered before the deductible.

Calendar year

Resets each January

Per cause

One per illness or injury

Family

Individual amounts up to a family cap

Carryover and common accident

Fourth-quarter credit; one deductible per accident

03

Coinsurance

After the deductible, the plan and the insured share covered charges by percentage. In an 80/20 plan the insurer pays 80 percent and the insured pays 20 percent. Coinsurance is applied to the allowed amount, not to whatever the provider bills, and out-of-network coinsurance is usually higher. Copayments differ from coinsurance: a copayment is a flat dollar amount per visit or prescription, often not subject to the deductible. Coinsurance keeps the insured interested in the cost of care; it also means a large claim can still leave a large bill, which is why the next provision exists.

Coinsurance

Percentage share after the deductible

Copayment

Flat dollar amount per service

04

Stop-loss and maximums

Think of the out-of-pocket maximum as a ceiling on what you pay for covered care. After you reach it, the plan pays 100 percent of covered in-network costs for the rest of the plan year. On a Marketplace plan, your deductible, coinsurance and copayments for covered in-network services count toward that limit. Your premiums and balance bills do not. Older plans also carried lifetime maximum benefits, such as one million dollars; those are now prohibited for essential health benefits. Limited policies that qualify for a federal exception may still cap benefits, which is another reason they are not substitutes for major medical.

Deductible

Insured pays first

Coinsurance

Shared until the out-of-pocket limit

Limit reached

Plan pays all covered in-network costs for the rest of the plan year

05

Work the numbers

Marcus’s plan has a 3,000 dollar deductible, 80/20 coinsurance and a 6,000 dollar out-of-pocket maximum. A hospital stay produces 20,000 dollars of allowed charges. He pays the first 3,000. Of the remaining 17,000 he owes 20 percent, which is 3,400, for a total of 6,400. But his out-of-pocket maximum is 6,000, so he pays 6,000 and the insurer pays 14,000. If a second claim arrives in December, the plan pays 100 percent because he has already reached the maximum. The arithmetic is always deductible first, coinsurance second, cap last.

06

Supplemental and comprehensive forms

When major medical is layered over a basic hospital plan, a corridor deductible sits between them: the basic plan pays first, the insured pays the corridor amount, then major medical pays with coinsurance. An integrated deductible instead counts what the basic plan paid toward the major medical deductible. A comprehensive major medical plan has no basic layer; one deductible and coinsurance structure covers everything. Understand both because exam questions still describe layered plans, while the policies sold today are nearly all comprehensive and must meet the federal rules on essential benefits and out-of-pocket limits.

Supplemental major medical

Basic plan, then corridor deductible, then major medical

Comprehensive major medical

One deductible and coinsurance for all services

More detail

What counts toward the out-of-pocket maximum

The federal annual limit counts deductibles, coinsurance and copayments for in-network essential health benefits. It does not count premiums, balance bills from out-of-network providers, or spending on services the plan does not cover. For 2026 the federal ceiling is $10,150 for self-only and $20,300 for family coverage; plans may set lower limits.

Example: Marcus’s $6,000 maximum is well below the federal ceiling, so his plan is more generous than the law requires.

Embedded versus aggregate family deductibles

An embedded family deductible lets one family member’s coverage begin once that person meets the individual deductible, even if the family total is not met. An aggregate deductible requires the whole family amount before anyone’s coverage begins. Federal rules require an embedded individual out-of-pocket limit within family plans.

Example: Elena meets her $3,000 individual deductible in March; her claims are covered even though the $6,000 family deductible is unmet.
