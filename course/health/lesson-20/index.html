01

Fixed indemnity plans

A fixed indemnity plan pays a stated dollar amount when a covered event occurs: 200 dollars per hospital day, 75 dollars per physician visit, 1,500 dollars per surgery. The payment is the same whether the bill is small or large, and it goes to the insured unless assigned. A fixed indemnity policy can qualify for an exception to some federal coverage rules if it meets the legal conditions. The important point for a buyer is simple: a fixed payment might cover only part of the bill. It does not replace comprehensive insurance. It can soften deductibles or lost wages for Elena’s coworker; it cannot replace her medical plan.

02

What a health service plan is

A nonprofit health service plan is a corporation organized not for profit to provide or pay for health services to its subscribers. Historically, hospital service plans paid hospitals and medical service plans paid physicians, under contracts with those providers. In Maryland they are licensed under Insurance Article Title 14 and regulated by the Insurance Administration like insurers, with additional obligations tied to their nonprofit mission. Today a service plan may offer indemnity, PPO and HMO products. The legal form, not the product menu, makes it a health service plan.

03

Plans offered and other services

Priya’s service plan quote lists several products: a traditional plan that pays providers directly, a PPO with network discounts and an HMO product through an affiliated organization. Service plans also provide what the outline calls other services: wellness programs, nurse lines, disease management and pharmacy benefit administration. Each product carries an evidence of coverage that describes benefits, exclusions and member cost sharing. Maryland mandated benefits apply to service plan contracts just as they apply to insurer policies, so the mental health parity, newborn and dependent rules you will study in Lesson 56 bind these plans too.

04

Participating providers and panels

The defining feature of a service plan is its contracts with providers. A participating, or qualified, provider agrees to accept the plan’s payment as payment in full for covered services, billing the subscriber only for copayments, deductibles or coinsurance. The list of contracted providers is the provider panel. When a subscriber uses a participating provider, the plan pays the provider directly and there is no balance billing. Nonparticipating providers may be paid at a lower level, and the subscriber may owe the difference. Choice of provider or pharmacy therefore has a price attached.

Nonparticipating

05

Disclosure to subscribers

Because subscribers depend on the panel, Maryland requires service plans and other carriers to disclose how the plan works: which providers participate, how to find a participating pharmacy, what happens out of network, how referrals and prior authorizations operate, and how to file a grievance or appeal. The evidence of coverage and the provider directory must be accurate and current. A subscriber who relies on a directory listing an in-network physician is protected if the listing was wrong. Theo should walk Priya’s employees through the directory before they pick a plan, because the network is the benefit.

06

Where each fits

Line the products up. Fixed indemnity pays the insured a set amount per event and is a supplement. A nonprofit health service plan is a provider-contracting organization that may offer several product types to subscribers. Participating providers accept plan payment in full; panels define choice; disclosure tells subscribers how to use the plan. In the next two lessons you will meet HMOs, which also contract with providers but add prepaid financing and a gatekeeper, and PPOs, which contract for discounts without a gatekeeper. The vocabulary from Lesson 18 will carry you through all of them.

More detail

Fixed indemnity is not minimum essential coverage

Holding only a fixed indemnity plan leaves a person without comprehensive coverage. Producers must not present it as satisfying any coverage requirement or as a substitute for a major medical plan, and the policy must carry the required limited-benefit notice.

Example: A $200-per-day hospital policy pays $1,000 for a five-day stay that cost $40,000; the insured owes the rest without major medical.

Assignment and direct payment

Under a service plan, payment to participating providers is built into the provider contract. Under an insurer’s policy, direct payment usually requires the insured to assign benefits. The result for the patient is similar; the mechanism differs.

Example: Priya’s employee shows her service plan card and the hospital bills the plan directly without a separate assignment form.
