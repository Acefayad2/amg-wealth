01

Financing and delivery in one organization

A health maintenance organization does two things that insurers and hospitals normally do separately. It collects a fixed prepaid amount for each member, and it arranges or directly provides the care those members need. Because it holds the money and runs the care, it has every incentive to keep members healthy and to manage how services are used. An HMO operates in a defined service area; members who travel outside it are generally covered only for emergencies and urgent care. In Maryland an HMO is authorized under the Health-General Article, holds a certificate of authority from the Insurance Commissioner and answers to the Department of Health on quality of care.

Fixed amount per member

Care delivered

Through the HMO’s providers

02

The gatekeeper

When Priya’s employee joins the HMO she selects a primary care physician, often called the gatekeeper. The PCP delivers routine and preventive care and decides when a referral to a specialist is needed. Without the referral, specialty care is not covered except for services the plan designates as open access, such as obstetrics and gynecology or, under Maryland law, certain direct-access situations. The gatekeeper model controls costs by making sure specialists see patients who need them. It also means the choice of PCP is the most important decision a member makes.

03

Copayments and prepaid financing

HMO members usually pay a flat copayment per visit or prescription rather than a deductible and coinsurance, and the plan pays the provider the rest. Many HMOs pay their physicians by capitation: a fixed monthly amount per assigned member, whether that member visits once or ten times. That is the prepaid basis carried down to the provider. Models differ. A staff model employs its physicians; a group model contracts with one medical group; an IPA model contracts with independent physicians who also see other patients; a network model contracts with several groups. All share prepaid financing and limited choice.

Capitation

04

Preventive, primary and referral care

HMOs built their reputation on prevention. Annual physicals, immunizations, screenings and well-child visits are covered, often with no copayment, because preventing illness lowers the plan’s costs. Primary care is delivered by the PCP; referral care is delivered by specialists on the PCP’s order. Hospital services, including inpatient admissions and surgery, are covered when authorized and delivered at participating hospitals. Other basic services include diagnostic laboratory and imaging, outpatient surgery, maternity care, mental health and substance use treatment at parity, and prescription drugs. Members are called subscribers or enrollees and hold an evidence of coverage.

Physicals, screenings, immunizations; low or no copay

05

Emergency care anywhere

Emergency care is the exception to every network rule. If Priya’s employee is injured on vacation in Ohio, the HMO must cover emergency services without a referral and without regard to the service area, judged by the prudent layperson standard: would a reasonable person believe the symptoms needed immediate attention? Federal and Maryland law require coverage of emergency care at the in-network cost sharing level and protect the member from surprise balance bills. Once stabilized, the member is expected to return to the network for follow-up care, and the HMO may arrange a transfer.

06

Reading an HMO question

The exam describes a plan and asks you to name it or to decide a claim. Look for the markers: prepaid premium, a primary care physician who must approve referrals, copayments rather than coinsurance, no coverage out of network except emergencies, a defined service area, members or subscribers rather than insureds. If a question says a member saw a specialist without a referral for a nonemergency, the HMO may deny. If it says the member had chest pain in another state, the HMO must pay. Remember Maryland’s regulatory split: certificate from the Insurance Commissioner, quality oversight from the Department of Health.

More detail

Open panel versus closed panel

A closed panel HMO, such as a staff or group model, uses physicians who serve only that plan’s members. An open panel HMO, such as an IPA model, contracts with physicians who also treat patients outside the plan. Lesson 22 uses the same terms for PPOs.

Example: Priya’s IPA-model HMO lets her employees keep a family doctor who also sees patients from other plans.

Maryland direct access rules

Maryland law requires HMOs to allow certain direct access without a referral, including to obstetric and gynecologic care, and to cover standing referrals for members with serious chronic conditions. These rules soften the gatekeeper model without removing it.

Example: A member may schedule an annual gynecologic exam without first visiting her PCP.
