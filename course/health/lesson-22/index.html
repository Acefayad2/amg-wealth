Explanations & examples

01

Preferred provider organizations

A PPO is a network of hospitals and physicians that have agreed to provide care to plan members at discounted fees. Members may see any provider, but the plan pays a higher share in network and a lower share out of network, and in-network providers accept the allowance as payment in full. There is no gatekeeper; members see specialists without referrals. PPOs are fee-for-service arrangements: the provider is paid per service at the negotiated rate. Marcus’s employer offers a PPO because it preserves choice while the network discounts hold down premiums.

In network

Higher benefits; discounted fees; no balance bill

Out of network

Lower benefits; possible balance bill

No gatekeeper

Specialists without referral

02

Open and closed panels

Panel describes who may join the network and whom the providers may treat. An open panel PPO or IPA-model HMO contracts with independent providers who also treat patients from other plans, and it admits any qualified provider willing to accept its terms. A closed panel, typical of staff and group model HMOs, uses providers who serve only that plan’s members. For members, open panels mean more choice and a better chance their current doctor participates. For plans, closed panels mean tighter control over practice patterns. Exclusive provider organizations, or EPOs, use a PPO-style network but cover nothing out of network except emergencies.

Open panel

Independent providers; broad admission

Closed panel

Providers exclusive to the plan

EPO

Network only; no referrals; no out-of-network benefits

03

Point-of-service plans

A point-of-service plan is an HMO with an escape hatch. Inside the network the member uses a primary care physician, obtains referrals and pays copayments, exactly as in an HMO. At the point of service, when care is needed, the member may instead go outside the network, and the plan pays indemnity-style benefits with a deductible and coinsurance, usually leaving the member with higher costs. The POS design answers the biggest objection to HMOs, loss of choice, while keeping the gatekeeper for most care. Members should understand that out-of-network use often requires them to file their own claims.

In network

PCP, referrals, copayments

Out of network

Deductible and coinsurance; member files claims

Decision point

Made each time care is sought

04

Choosing among the designs

Priya asks Theo which design her bakery should offer. He frames the trade-off. An HMO costs least and restricts most. A PPO costs more and lets members see specialists anywhere, with incentives to stay in network. A POS sits between, with a gatekeeper in network and coverage out. An EPO looks like a PPO but pays nothing out of network. The right answer depends on the workforce: employees with established specialists value PPO freedom; younger employees may prefer the HMO premium. Whatever the design, Maryland’s mandated benefits and the federal essential health benefit rules apply.

HMO

Lowest cost; network only; gatekeeper

POS

Gatekeeper in network; coverage out of network

PPO

Higher cost; two benefit levels; no gatekeeper

EPO

Network only; no gatekeeper

05

TRICARE

TRICARE is the Department of Defense health program for active duty service members, retirees, National Guard and Reserve members and their families. Its options mirror civilian designs. TRICARE Prime works like an HMO, with a primary care manager, referrals and low out-of-pocket costs, and is mandatory for active duty members. TRICARE Select works like a PPO, allowing self-referral to network providers with cost sharing. TRICARE For Life supplements Medicare for retirees 65 and older, paying after Medicare. A private health policy generally excludes care TRICARE furnishes, which is the government plan exclusion you saw in Lesson 9.

TRICARE Prime

HMO-style; primary care manager

TRICARE Select

PPO-style; self-referral with cost sharing

TRICARE For Life

Pays after Medicare for retirees 65 and older

06

Name the plan from its rules

Four clues name the plan. Does the member need a referral for a specialist? Yes points to an HMO or the in-network side of a POS. Is there coverage out of network? Yes points to a PPO or POS; no points to an HMO or EPO. Does the member pay copayments or deductible and coinsurance? Copayments lean HMO; coinsurance leans PPO; both appear in a POS depending on where care was received. Is the payer the Department of Defense? Then it is TRICARE, and its option follows the same referral and network clues.

Referral required

HMO or POS in network

Out-of-network benefits

PPO or POS

Network only, no referral

EPO

Military families

TRICARE

More detail

Preferred providers and balance billing

A preferred provider contractually accepts the plan’s negotiated fee as payment in full, so an in-network member owes only the deductible, coinsurance or copayment. An out-of-network provider has no such contract and may bill the difference between its charge and the plan’s allowance, subject to federal surprise-billing protections for emergencies and certain facility-based care.

Example: Marcus’s in-network knee surgery costs him 20% of the negotiated $8,000 fee; the same surgery out of network could cost 40% of the allowance plus a balance bill.

Maryland Medicare Advantage HMOs and PPOs

The HMO and PPO designs also appear in Medicare Advantage plans, which Lesson 43 covers. The network rules are the same; the payer is Medicare through a private carrier.

Example: Dennis’s Medicare Advantage HMO requires a primary care physician just as Priya’s commercial HMO does.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Describe preferred provider organizations and distinguish open and closed panels.
  • Explain how a point-of-service plan combines HMO and indemnity features.
  • Identify TRICARE’s purpose and its main options.
