01

Cheaper settings, same care

A hospital bed is the most expensive place to receive care, so plans encourage alternatives. Preventive care catches problems early. Outpatient and ambulatory surgery centers handle procedures that once required admission. Home health care, skilled nursing facilities and hospice care let patients leave the hospital sooner. Urgent care centers and telehealth visits replace emergency rooms for minor problems. Plans steer members toward these settings with lower copayments and sometimes refuse to pay for inpatient care that could safely have been given elsewhere. Marcus’s outpatient knee surgery costs a fraction of an overnight stay.

02

Utilization review

Utilization management checks whether care is necessary and appropriate. Prospective review happens before care: precertification or prior authorization for a planned admission, surgery or costly drug. Concurrent review happens during care: a nurse reviewer monitors a hospital stay and approves continued days. Retrospective review happens after care, examining claims for appropriateness. Second surgical opinion programs and case management for complex conditions belong to the same family. In Maryland, utilization review decisions that deny care are adverse decisions subject to the grievance and appeal process in Lesson 27.

03

Why coordinate benefits

Marcus is covered under his employer’s plan and, as a dependent, under Elena’s. Without a rule, their son’s 4,000 dollar emergency visit could be paid twice. Coordination of benefits, a provision in group plans, decides which plan is primary and limits the total paid to 100 percent of the allowable expense. The primary plan pays as if it were the only coverage. The secondary plan pays the remaining allowable expense up to what it would have paid alone. The family never collects more than the bill, which honors the principle of indemnity.

Primary plan

Pays first, as if alone

Secondary plan

Pays the balance up to its own benefit

Total

Never more than 100% of allowable expense

04

Order of benefits

The rules are mechanical. A plan covering a person as an employee or subscriber is primary over a plan covering that person as a dependent. For a dependent child of married parents, the birthday rule applies: the plan of the parent whose birthday falls earlier in the calendar year is primary, regardless of age. If the parents share a birthday, the plan in force longer is primary. For divorced or separated parents, the custodial parent’s plan is primary unless a court decree assigns responsibility. A plan covering a person as an active employee is primary over a plan covering a retiree or laid-off employee.

Employee over dependent

Your own plan pays first for you

Birthday rule

Parent with the earlier birthday pays first for children

Custody or decree

Custodial parent first unless a court says otherwise

05

Work a family claim

Marcus was born in September; Elena was born in March. Their son’s emergency visit produces 4,000 dollars of allowable charges. Elena’s plan is primary because her birthday is earlier in the year. It pays 80 percent after her 500 dollar deductible: 2,800 dollars. Marcus’s plan is secondary. It would have paid 3,200 dollars alone, so it pays the remaining 1,200 dollars, and the family owes nothing. If Marcus’s plan were an individual policy rather than a group plan, coordination of benefits would not apply; the insurance-with-other-companies provision from Lesson 17 would prorate instead.

06

Containment plus coordination

Cost containment and coordination attack waste from two directions. Containment reduces what the whole system spends by moving care to appropriate settings and reviewing its necessity before, during and after. Coordination prevents duplicate payment when two plans cover one person. Both rely on information: precertification requires the member or provider to call ahead; coordination requires the member to disclose other coverage on claim forms. A member who hides a spouse’s plan or skips precertification may face reduced benefits. Producers should explain both duties at enrollment so members use their plans correctly.

More detail

Medicare as a coordinating payer

When an active employee age 65 or older is covered by an employer plan with 20 or more employees, the employer plan is primary and Medicare is secondary. For employers with fewer than 20 employees, Medicare is primary. Lesson 46 develops these Medicare secondary payer rules.

Example: Dennis keeps working at 66 for a 60-employee firm; his group plan pays first and Medicare second.

Precertification penalties

Plans may reduce benefits when a member fails to obtain required precertification for nonemergency admissions, but Maryland rules bar penalties for emergency care and require that a medical necessity denial be made by appropriate clinical personnel with appeal rights.

Example: A planned surgery admitted without precertification may carry a $500 benefit reduction; an emergency appendectomy may not.
