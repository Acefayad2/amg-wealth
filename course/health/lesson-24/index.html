Explanations & examples

01

What HIPAA set out to fix

Before 1996, a worker who changed jobs could face a new waiting period for conditions she already had, and a small employer could be turned down for coverage because one employee was sick. The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act addressed both. It limited pre-existing condition exclusions in group plans and gave credit for prior coverage, required insurers to issue coverage to small employers and renew group coverage, and created special enrollment rights. It also produced the privacy and security rules that govern how health information is handled. The Affordable Care Act later went further, but HIPAA’s framework remains on the exam.

02

Eligibility and special enrollment

HIPAA forbids a group health plan from basing eligibility or premium contributions on health status, medical history, genetic information, disability or claims experience. Everyone in the eligible class gets in on the same terms. HIPAA also created special enrollment periods: an employee who declined coverage may enroll midyear within 30 days after losing other coverage, or after marriage, birth, adoption or placement for adoption. Later law added 60 days for gaining or losing Medicaid or Children’s Health Insurance Program eligibility. Priya’s employee who waived coverage because of a spouse’s plan may join within 30 days if the spouse loses that plan.

03

Guaranteed issue

HIPAA required insurers in the small group market, then defined as 2 to 50 employees, to accept every small employer that applied and every eligible employee of that employer, regardless of health. It also defined eligible individuals, people leaving group coverage after at least 18 months with no gap of 63 days or more who had exhausted any COBRA, and required that they be able to obtain individual coverage; Maryland met that duty through a state high-risk pool before 2014. The Affordable Care Act extended guaranteed issue to all comprehensive individual and small group coverage, so today no one is denied for health reasons.

Guaranteed issue for small employers; eligible individuals leaving group plans

ACA

Guaranteed issue for all comprehensive individual and small group coverage

04

Pre-existing conditions and creditable coverage

Before the Affordable Care Act, HIPAA let group plans limit coverage for certain pre-existing conditions. The look-back was six months. An exclusion could last up to 12 months, or 18 for a late enrollee. Prior coverage could shorten that wait day for day if there was no break of 63 days or more. Plans issued certificates to document that creditable coverage. Pregnancy was protected. So were children enrolled within 30 days of birth, adoption or placement for adoption. The rule today is simpler: comprehensive group plans cannot exclude a condition just because you had it before you enrolled.

05

Guaranteed renewability

HIPAA requires insurers to renew group and individual coverage at the policyholder’s option. Nonrenewal is allowed only for nonpayment of premium, fraud or intentional misrepresentation, failure to meet participation or contribution rules in a group plan, movement outside the service area, or the insurer’s withdrawal from the entire market with 180 days’ notice. An insurer may not drop Priya’s group because claims ran high. Premiums may still change at renewal within the rating rules. This is the guaranteed renewable clause from Lesson 17 written into federal law for medical coverage.

At the policyholder’s option

Exceptions

Nonpayment, fraud, participation rules, service area, market exit

Premiums

06

HIPAA then and now

Think of HIPAA as the floor the Affordable Care Act built on. HIPAA said group plans could not pick and choose employees by health, had to credit prior coverage against limited pre-existing exclusions, had to accept small employers and had to renew. The ACA removed pre-existing exclusions and health underwriting from comprehensive coverage altogether and extended guaranteed issue to individuals. HIPAA’s special enrollment rights, renewability rules and privacy protections remain in force unchanged. When a question describes a group plan refusing a sick employee or excluding a condition for 12 months, the answer is that HIPAA, and now the ACA, forbid it.

More detail

The privacy and security rules

HIPAA’s administrative simplification provisions require covered entities, including health plans and producers acting for them, to protect individually identifiable health information, use it only for treatment, payment and operations without authorization, and give individuals access to their records. Producers who handle applications and claims are bound by these rules through their agreements with carriers.

Example: Theo may not discuss Rosa’s medical history with her employer without her written authorization.

Late enrollees

A late enrollee is someone who joins a group plan outside the initial enrollment period and outside any special enrollment period. Under HIPAA a plan could apply the longer 18-month exclusion to late enrollees; today plans may still make late enrollees wait for the next open enrollment, though not exclude conditions.

Example: An employee who waived coverage at hire and has no qualifying event must wait until open enrollment to join.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Explain HIPAA eligibility rules and special enrollment rights in group plans.
  • Describe guaranteed issue and guaranteed renewability under HIPAA.
  • Apply the creditable coverage and pre-existing condition rules and explain how the Affordable Care Act changed them.
