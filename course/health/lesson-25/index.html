01

The high-deductible plan comes first

Rosa wants a health savings account. The law says she may have one only if she is covered by a high-deductible health plan and by no other disqualifying coverage. For 2026 an HDHP must have a deductible of at least 1,700 dollars for self-only coverage or 3,400 dollars for family coverage, and its out-of-pocket maximum may not exceed 8,500 dollars self-only or 17,000 dollars family. The plan may cover preventive care before the deductible. These numbers are indexed each year, so learn the structure and check the current figures before advising a client.

02

Who may contribute

To contribute to an HSA, Rosa must be covered by an HDHP on the first day of the month, must not have other first-dollar medical coverage such as a spouse’s traditional plan or a general purpose flexible spending account, must not be enrolled in Medicare, and must not be claimed as a dependent on someone else’s tax return. Permitted other coverage includes dental, vision, disability, long-term care and specific-disease policies. Dennis, who enrolls in Medicare at 65, must stop contributing, although he may keep spending what he saved. The account belongs to the individual, not the employer.

03

Contribution limits and tax treatment

For 2026 the annual HSA contribution limit is 4,400 dollars for self-only coverage and 8,750 dollars for family coverage, from all sources combined, with an additional 1,000 dollars for account holders age 55 or older. Contributions are deductible, or excluded from income when made through payroll. Earnings grow tax-free. Withdrawals for qualified medical expenses, including deductibles, coinsurance, prescriptions, dental and vision care, are tax-free. Withdrawals for anything else are taxed as income plus a 20 percent penalty, which disappears after age 65, death or disability. Unused balances roll over forever and move with the account holder.

04

Health reimbursement arrangements

An HRA is different in kind. Only the employer funds it; employees may not contribute. The employer owns the account and sets what it reimburses, typically deductibles, coinsurance and other medical expenses, and whether unused amounts carry over. Reimbursements are tax-free to the employee. An HRA need not be paired with an HDHP. Unlike an HSA, it usually does not travel with an employee who leaves. Newer variants let employers reimburse individual market premiums: the individual coverage HRA for any employer and the qualified small employer HRA for employers with fewer than 50 employees that offer no group plan.

05

HSA versus HRA

Put them side by side. The HSA is the individual’s account: funded by employee, employer or both; requires an HDHP; capped by the annual limit; portable; invested; subject to the 20 percent penalty on nonmedical use. The HRA is the employer’s promise: funded only by the employer; pairs with any plan; limited only by the employer’s design, except the QSEHRA cap; stays with the employer; no investment account; no nonmedical withdrawals at all. Priya might offer an HDHP with HSA contributions to her bakery staff, or keep a traditional plan and add an HRA to soften its deductible.

Employee owns; HDHP required; annual limit; portable

HRA

06

Advising Rosa and Priya

Rosa, self-employed and healthy, buys a 2026 HDHP with a 3,000 dollar deductible and opens an HSA. She may contribute up to 4,400 dollars, deduct it, and pay her deductible from the account tax-free; what she does not spend keeps growing for retirement medical costs. Priya, employing twenty people, compares an HDHP with a 1,500 dollar employer HSA contribution against a traditional plan with a 2,000 dollar HRA. Both lower the employees’ exposure; only the HSA money belongs to the employees permanently. Lesson 57 adds flexible spending accounts and the tax rules that tie these accounts together.

Lesson 57

FSAs and the tax rules

More detail

Last-month rule and mid-year changes

A person who is HSA-eligible on December 1 may contribute the full annual limit for that year under the last-month rule, provided eligibility continues through the following year. Otherwise contributions are prorated monthly. Employer contributions count toward the same limit.

Example: Rosa starts HDHP coverage on July 1 and stays enrolled through next December; she may contribute the full $4,400 for 2026.

Limited-purpose FSAs preserve HSA eligibility

A general purpose health flexible spending account is disqualifying coverage for an HSA, but a limited-purpose FSA restricted to dental and vision expenses is not. Employers offering HDHPs often make that change so employees can hold both.

Example: Marcus keeps a dental-and-vision FSA at work while contributing to the HSA attached to his family HDHP.
