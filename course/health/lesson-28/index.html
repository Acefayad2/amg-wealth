01

Why the definition matters

Rosa, an electrician, injures her shoulder and can no longer climb or pull wire. She could manage a hardware store or estimate jobs from a desk. Is she disabled? The answer depends entirely on how her policy defines the word. Disability income insurance does not pay because a person is hurt; it pays because the person meets the policy’s definition of total or partial disability. Two policies with identical benefit amounts can produce opposite claim decisions on the same injury. Before quoting any disability policy, a producer reads the definition and explains it in the client’s own terms.

02

Own occupation

An own-occupation definition pays when the insured cannot perform the material and substantial duties of her regular occupation, even if she could work at something else. Under a true own-occupation policy Rosa is totally disabled the day she cannot work as an electrician, and she may earn income in another job while collecting full benefits. A modified own-occupation definition pays only if she is also not working elsewhere. Own-occupation coverage is the most favorable and the most expensive, and insurers reserve it for professionals and skilled trades with definable duties.

True own occupation

Cannot do your job; may work elsewhere and still collect

Modified own occupation

Cannot do your job and not working elsewhere

03

Any occupation

An any-occupation definition pays only when the insured cannot perform the duties of any occupation for which she is reasonably suited by education, training and experience. Rosa, able to manage a store, would not be totally disabled under this test. It is the strictest definition, resembles the Social Security standard, and costs the least. Many policies and most group long-term disability plans use a split definition: own occupation for the first two years, then any occupation. A producer must warn clients that benefits can end at the two-year mark if they could do other suitable work.

Any occupation

Cannot do any suitable work; strictest, cheapest

Split definition

Own occupation for 2 years, then any occupation

04

Income replacement contracts

A third approach drops the occupational test. A pure loss of income, or income replacement, contract pays based on what the insured actually loses. If Rosa’s shoulder cuts her income from 6,000 dollars a month to 3,000 dollars, the policy pays a benefit proportional to the 50 percent loss, whether she stopped working or changed jobs. These contracts need no definition of total disability for most claims, because they measure dollars rather than duties. They suit self-employed people whose income varies and whose disability may be partial from the start. Lesson 30 shows the residual arithmetic in detail.

05

Presumptive disability

Some losses are so severe that the policy presumes total disability without applying the occupational test. The usual list is total and permanent loss of sight in both eyes, hearing in both ears, speech, or the use of two limbs. If Rosa loses both hands in an accident she is presumed totally disabled, benefits begin, often without the elimination period, and they continue for the full benefit period even if she later earns income. Presumptive disability is a floor that guarantees payment in the worst cases; everything short of the list still runs through the policy definition.

Hearing or speech

Limbs

06

Under a physician’s care

Policies require the insured to be under the regular care of a physician for the condition causing the disability. The requirement prevents claims for conditions nobody is treating and lets the insurer verify continuing disability through medical records and periodic proof. Most modern policies waive it when care would not improve the condition, so a paralyzed insured need not schedule pointless visits. Rosa must keep her orthopedic appointments while she claims, and her physician’s reports will support each monthly benefit. Put the pieces together: definition met, care maintained, elimination period served, benefit paid.

More detail

Total disability is defined, not diagnosed

A physician certifies the medical condition and its limitations; the insurer applies the policy definition to those limitations. The same diagnosis can be a total disability for a surgeon and no disability at all for a telephone dispatcher.

Example: A hand tremor ends a surgeon’s career under own occupation but may not disable an accountant under any definition.

Recurrent disability

Most policies treat a disability that returns within six months of recovery as a continuation of the original claim, with no new elimination period. A longer gap starts a new claim and a new elimination period.

Example: Rosa returns to work for four months, then her shoulder fails again; benefits resume immediately under the recurrent disability provision.
