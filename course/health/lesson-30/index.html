01

Partial disability

Rosa recovers enough to work half days but cannot do the heavy parts of her trade. A partial disability benefit pays a flat percentage of the total disability benefit, commonly 50 percent, for a limited time, often six months. Traditional partial disability benefits require that the insured first was totally disabled and then returned to work part time or in a reduced capacity. If Rosa’s total benefit is 3,600 dollars, her partial benefit is 1,800 dollars a month for up to six months. The flat percentage is simple but ignores how much income she actually lost.

50%

02

Residual disability

A residual disability benefit replaces the lost share of income instead of a flat percentage. The formula is the percentage of income lost, multiplied by the total disability benefit. Rosa earned 6,000 dollars; working half days she earns 3,600, a 40 percent loss. Her residual benefit is 40 percent of 3,600, which is 1,440 dollars a month, for as long as the income loss continues within the benefit period. Most residual provisions require at least a 20 percent income loss to pay and treat a loss of 75 or 80 percent as total. Residual benefits do not require a prior period of total disability.

03

Partial versus residual

The two provisions answer the same situation differently. Partial pays a fixed fraction for a short time after total disability. Residual pays a proportion that tracks actual income loss for as long as the loss lasts, and it can begin without any total disability. Residual coverage costs more and suits people whose disabilities tend to be gradual or whose return to work is slow, like Rosa. Income replacement contracts from Lesson 28 are built entirely around the residual idea. On a calculation question, identify which provision the policy uses before touching the numbers.

Partial

Flat %, often 50%; limited months; after total disability

Residual

Proportional to income loss; full benefit period; no prior total disability needed

04

Additional monthly benefit

Social Security disability takes months to approve and has a five-month waiting period. An additional monthly benefit rider pays an extra amount, say 1,200 dollars, during the first six to twelve months of a disability, when the insured is waiting to learn whether Social Security will pay. It is a short bridge, not a permanent increase. If Rosa’s base benefit is 3,600 dollars and her AMB rider is 1,200, she receives 4,800 a month for the first year, then 3,600 whether or not Social Security approves her. The rider’s premium reflects its short duration.

Months 1 to 12

Base benefit plus additional monthly benefit

After month 12

Base benefit only

Purpose

05

Social insurance supplement

A social insurance supplement rider also adds a benefit, but its payment depends on what Social Security does. If Social Security denies the claim, the full SIS benefit is paid. If Social Security pays, the SIS benefit is reduced or eliminated dollar for dollar. The insured is protected against the strict federal definition without paying for a permanent benefit that duplicates Social Security. Group long-term disability plans use the same idea in reverse: they pay a percentage of salary minus Social Security and workers compensation benefits, so the employee’s total income stays near the target percentage.

SIS rider

Pays if Social Security denies; offsets if it pays

Group LTD offset

Benefit reduced by Social Security and workers compensation

06

Fitting the pieces together

Picture Rosa’s claim over two years. Months one through three: elimination period, no benefit. Months four through twelve: base benefit of 3,600 plus an additional monthly benefit of 1,200. Month twelve: Social Security approves 1,800 a month, so her SIS rider pays nothing, while the AMB rider ends on schedule. Month eighteen: she returns half time with a 40 percent income loss, and her residual benefit pays 1,440 until the loss ends or she reaches 65. Each provision solves one problem: the wait, the federal gap, the partial return. A good policy design anticipates all three.

More detail

Minimum and maximum loss thresholds

Residual provisions usually require an income loss of at least 20 percent before paying and treat a loss of 75 or 80 percent or more as total disability, paying the full benefit. Below the threshold, no benefit; above it, full benefit.

Example: If Rosa’s income falls only 15 percent, her residual rider pays nothing; if it falls 80 percent, she receives the full $3,600.

Why individual policies are rarely offset

Individual disability policies usually pay their stated benefit regardless of Social Security, because the insured bought a fixed amount. Riders like SIS add an offset only to the supplemental layer. Group plans, which replace a percentage of salary, almost always offset other sources.

Example: Rosa’s $3,600 base benefit is paid in full even after Social Security approves her; only the SIS layer is reduced.
