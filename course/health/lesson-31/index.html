01

Cost-of-living adjustment

Rosa’s 3,600 dollar benefit is fixed. If she is disabled for twenty years, inflation could cut its buying power in half. A cost-of-living adjustment rider increases the benefit during a claim, usually after the first twelve months of disability, by a fixed percentage or by the change in the consumer price index, often with a cap. It does not raise the benefit before a claim begins. The rider is one of the more expensive additions because long claims are exactly the ones it grows. For a 41-year-old with benefits to 65, Theo recommends it.

Year 1 of claim

$3,600 fixed

Each later year

Benefit rises by CPI or a set percentage

Purpose

Protect buying power during a long disability

02

Future increase option

Rosa expects her income to grow. A future increase option, also called a guaranteed insurability rider, lets her buy additional monthly benefit at specified option dates or ages without medical underwriting. The insurer may still require proof of higher income, because benefits must stay within issue limits. The rider protects against the day a new diagnosis makes her uninsurable. Without it, each increase would require a new application and full underwriting. Option dates are usually annual or every few years until a stated age, and unused options may expire.

Option dates

Buy more benefit on schedule

No medical underwriting

Health changes do not block the increase

Financial proof

Income must support the new amount

03

Annual renewable term and related provisions

An annual renewable term rider adds temporary monthly benefit at a premium that rises each year, like term life insurance. It suits a young insured who needs a higher benefit now at low initial cost and expects to convert it to level-premium coverage later. Two provisions from Lesson 17 reappear in disability policies: relation of earnings to insurance, which trims total benefits to actual earnings if the insured becomes overinsured, and change of occupation, which adjusts benefits when the insured moves to a more or less hazardous job. Both keep the benefit tied to real economic loss.

Annual renewable term rider

Extra benefit; premium steps up yearly; convertible

Relation of earnings

Benefits capped at earnings

Change of occupation

Benefits adjusted to the new job’s rate

04

Other cash benefits

Disability policies often carry lump-sum or expense benefits beside the monthly check. An accidental death and dismemberment benefit pays a principal sum for accidental death and a capital sum for loss of a limb or sight. A rehabilitation benefit pays for vocational training or retraining that helps the insured return to work, in addition to monthly benefits, sometimes up to a multiple of the monthly amount. A medical reimbursement benefit pays medical expenses for an injury that does not disable the insured, up to a stated amount, so a minor accident produces something even without lost income.

AD&D

Principal and capital sums

Rehabilitation

Pays for retraining to return to work

Medical reimbursement

Expenses for a nondisabling injury

05

Return of premium and cash value

Some policies promise money back. A return of premium rider refunds a percentage of premiums paid, less claims, at set intervals such as every ten years or at a stated age, provided the policy stays in force. It can raise the premium by half or more, and the refund is really the insured’s own money returned without interest. A cash value benefit, found in a few disability contracts, accumulates a surrender value like permanent life insurance. Both appeal to buyers who dislike paying for something they never use; Theo explains that the same dollars invested elsewhere might do better.

Return of premium

Refund of premiums less claims at intervals; costly

Cash value benefit

Accumulates a surrender value; rare

06

Designing Rosa’s policy

Rosa’s finished policy: 3,600 dollars a month, 90-day elimination, benefits to 65, true own occupation, residual benefit, waiver of premium, 24-hour coverage. Riders: cost-of-living adjustment so a long claim keeps pace with prices; future increase option so she can add benefit as her business grows; a rehabilitation benefit so retraining is funded; a modest medical reimbursement for small injuries. She declines return of premium as too expensive for what it returns. Each choice answers a specific risk, and each has a price the producer should state plainly before she signs.

COLA

Inflation during a claim

FIO

Growth without new underwriting

Rehabilitation and medical

Return to work; small injuries

Return of premium

Declined as poor value

More detail

COLA versus FIO

Cost-of-living adjustments operate only after a claim begins and raise the benefit being paid. Future increase options operate before a claim and raise the benefit that would be paid. A client who confuses them may think inflation protection covers the pre-disability years; it does not.

Example: Rosa’s COLA does nothing while she is healthy; her FIO lets her raise the benefit from $3,600 to $4,500 when her income grows.

Rehabilitation serves both parties

Insurers fund rehabilitation because every insured who returns to work ends a claim early. Most policies make rehabilitation voluntary and require insurer approval of the program.

Example: The insurer pays for Rosa’s electrical inspector certification course, which returns her to work within her trade’s industry.
