01

Group versus individual

Priya adds disability coverage for her twenty employees. A group plan differs from Rosa’s individual policy in predictable ways. The employer holds the master contract and often pays the premium. There is no individual medical underwriting; everyone in the class is covered. Benefits are a percentage of salary rather than a chosen dollar amount, with a monthly maximum. Coverage is usually nonoccupational and offsets Social Security and workers compensation. Definitions tend to be split: own occupation for two years, then any occupation. If the employer pays the premium, benefits are taxable. Coverage ends when employment ends, though some plans allow conversion.

Group

Employer contract; no individual underwriting; % of salary; offsets; ends with job

Individual

Owned by insured; underwritten; fixed amount; portable

02

Short-term disability

Short-term disability replaces income during the first weeks of a disability. Typical plans pay 60 to 70 percent of weekly salary for 13 to 26 weeks, sometimes 52. The elimination period is short, often zero days for accidents and seven for sickness, so benefits begin almost at once. Many employers self-fund STD because claims are frequent, small and predictable, hiring an insurer only to administer them. STD usually dovetails with sick leave, beginning when paid sick days run out. For Priya’s bakery, STD keeps a worker recovering from surgery on the payroll for the three months it takes to heal.

03

Long-term disability

Long-term disability begins where STD ends. The elimination period is 90 or 180 days, matched to the STD benefit period so the two plans hand off without a gap. Benefits replace 50 to 67 percent of salary up to a monthly cap, reduced by Social Security, workers compensation and other income, and continue to age 65 or the Social Security normal retirement age. The definition is usually split at 24 months. Pre-existing condition limits are common, for example excluding conditions treated in the three months before coverage if disability begins within the first twelve months. LTD is almost always insured rather than self-funded.

04

Key employee disability

Priya’s head baker developed the recipes that built the business. If he is disabled, revenue falls and a replacement must be recruited and trained. Key employee disability income insurance pays the business a monthly benefit or lump sum while the key person is disabled. The business is the applicant, owner, premium payer and beneficiary; the key employee is the insured. The benefit funds a temporary replacement, covers lost profits and buys time. Premiums are not deductible, and benefits are received tax-free. The key person’s own disability income is a separate matter, handled by his personal or group coverage.

05

Business overhead expense

If Rosa, a sole proprietor, is disabled, her income stops but the shop rent, utilities, employee wages, insurance premiums and loan payments continue. A business overhead expense policy reimburses those actual fixed expenses while the owner is disabled, up to a monthly maximum, for a short benefit period of one or two years, after a short elimination period, often 30 days. It does not pay the owner’s own salary or lost profit; her personal disability policy does that. BOE premiums are tax-deductible as a business expense, and benefits are taxable, but the expenses they pay are deductible, which nets to little tax.

Pays

Rent, utilities, staff wages, loan payments, premiums

06

Disability buyout

Priya and her partner agree in writing that if either is permanently disabled, the other will buy her share. A disability buyout policy funds that purchase. After a long elimination period, typically one or two years so that the disability is clearly permanent, the policy pays a lump sum or installments to the business or the healthy partner, who uses it to buy out the disabled owner. Premiums are not deductible and benefits are tax-free. The arrangement gives the disabled owner cash for her interest and gives the business a clean transfer, mirroring the life insurance funded buy-sell agreement.

More detail

Why LTD elimination periods match STD benefit periods

Employers set the LTD elimination period equal to the STD benefit period so an employee moves from one benefit to the next without an uncovered gap. A 26-week STD plan pairs with a 180-day LTD elimination period.

Example: Priya’s plan pays STD for 13 weeks and LTD after a 90-day elimination period.

Three business policies, three purposes

Key employee coverage replaces what the business loses when a valuable person cannot work. Business overhead expense keeps the lights on while an owner is disabled. Disability buyout funds the transfer of a permanently disabled owner’s interest. A single business may need all three.

Example: Priya’s bakery insures the head baker as a key person, carries BOE for its fixed costs and holds a buyout policy under the partnership agreement.
