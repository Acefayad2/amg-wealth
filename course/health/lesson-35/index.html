01

The master policy and the certificate

Priya signs the group health contract for her bakery. That document is the master policy, and Priya, the employer, is the policyholder. Her employees are not parties to the contract. Each covered employee receives a certificate of coverage, sometimes called a certificate of insurance or evidence of coverage, describing the benefits, exclusions, cost sharing and how to file a claim. The certificate is a summary; the master policy controls if they differ. Dependents are covered through the employee’s certificate. When a question asks who the insured is in group coverage, the answer is the employee; who owns the policy, the employer.

02

What the certificate must say

Because the certificate is all most employees ever see, Maryland and federal law require it to be accurate and complete. It must describe the benefits and limitations, the conditions for eligibility, how coverage ends and the continuation and conversion rights that follow. Maryland’s continuation statutes specifically require each certificate to explain how a surviving spouse, a divorced spouse or a terminated employee may elect continued coverage. The Affordable Care Act added the summary of benefits and coverage, a standardized four-page comparison document. A certificate that misstates benefits exposes the insurer to claims for the benefits described.

03

Experience and community rating

How is the group’s premium set? Experience rating bases next year’s premium on this group’s own claims history, adjusted for trend and expenses; it is used for large groups whose claims are statistically credible. Community rating charges all groups in an area the same rate regardless of claims. Federal and Maryland law now require modified community rating for small employers: premiums may vary only by age within a 3 to 1 band, tobacco use, geographic rating area and family composition, never by the group’s health or claims. Priya’s twenty-person bakery is community rated; a 2,000-employee hospital is experience rated.

Experience rating

Large groups; premium follows the group’s own claims

Modified community rating

Small groups; age, tobacco, area, family only

04

Why only true groups

Group insurance works because the group formed for some purpose other than buying insurance. Employees come together to work, union members to bargain, borrowers to borrow. That natural purpose means the group’s health is roughly average and no one joined because they were sick. A group assembled solely to buy insurance would attract the people who most need it, defeating the pricing. State laws therefore define the eligible groups, require a minimum size in some cases, and in Maryland permit true small employers down to a single eligible employee through the small group market and SHOP.

05

The eligible groups

Employer groups cover the employees of a single employer, the most common form. Creditor groups cover borrowers; the lender is policyholder and the coverage pays the debt, as in credit disability. Labor unions cover members, often through trusts the union and employers fund jointly. Multiple employer trusts pool small employers, usually in the same industry, so they can buy coverage on better terms; Maryland regulates these multiple employer welfare arrangements closely because some have failed. Associations formed for purposes other than insurance, typically professional or trade associations in existence for at least two years, may cover members. Credit unions cover their members.

06

Sorting a group question

When a question names an organization, ask two things. Was it formed for a purpose other than insurance? A trade association that has existed for years and happens to offer a health plan qualifies; a club organized last month to get cheap insurance does not. Who holds the master policy and who receives certificates? In an employer group it is the employer and employees; in a creditor group, the lender and borrowers; in an association, the association and members. Then ask how the premium is set: by the group’s own experience if large, by modified community rating if it is a small employer.

Multiple employer welfare arrangements

A MEWA offers health benefits to employees of two or more unrelated employers. Fully insured MEWAs are regulated as insurance; self-funded MEWAs must meet Maryland solvency and registration rules because several have collapsed leaving unpaid claims. Producers should verify a MEWA’s authorization before placing a client.

Example: A bakers’ trade trust that insures employees of forty small bakeries must be authorized in Maryland and may not operate as an unlicensed insurer.

Participation and contribution

Insurers require a minimum share of eligible employees to enroll, often 75 percent of those not covered elsewhere, and a minimum employer contribution, often 50 percent of the employee premium. Both reduce adverse selection and are discussed in Lesson 36.

Example: Priya must enroll at least 15 of her 20 eligible employees and pay half of each employee’s premium to meet the carrier’s rules.
