01

Advertising to groups

Theo markets a group plan to Priya and then helps her explain it to employees. The same truth-in-advertising rules apply: materials must be accurate, name the insurer, and present limitations as clearly as benefits. Enrollment meetings, brochures and the certificate itself are advertising for this purpose, and the insurer is responsible for them. Employer communications that oversell the plan can create liability for benefits the plan does not provide. The summary of benefits and coverage uses a standard format precisely so employees can compare plans without marketing spin.

Same rules

Truthful; insurer named; limits disclosed

Scope

Brochures, meetings, certificates

Standard document

Summary of benefits and coverage

02

Whose law governs

Group contracts cross state lines. The general rule is that the law of the state where the master policy is delivered governs the contract, even if certificate holders live elsewhere. An employer headquartered in Delaware with a Maryland branch may hold a Delaware master policy. Maryland protects its residents by extending certain mandated benefits and consumer protections to certificates delivered to Maryland residents under out-of-state group policies, and by requiring out-of-state carriers to be authorized here. When a question asks which state’s rules apply, start with the place of delivery, then check for extraterritorial provisions.

03

Underwriting the group

An insurer underwriting Priya’s bakery does not examine twenty people. It examines the group. Characteristics of the group include its size, industry, age and gender mix, location and turnover; a stable workforce in a low-hazard industry prices well. Plan design matters: rich benefits with no employee contribution invite heavier use than a plan with cost sharing. Persistency, the group’s history of staying with carriers, signals whether the insurer will recover its setup costs. Administrative capability, the employer’s ability to handle payroll deductions, enrollments and records, affects expense. For small groups, health status may not be used at all.

04

Participation requirements

The insurer requires a minimum share of eligible employees to enroll. In a noncontributory plan, where the employer pays the entire premium, 100 percent of eligible employees must participate, because there is no reason for anyone to decline. In a contributory plan, where employees pay part, carriers typically require about 75 percent of eligible employees who do not have other coverage to enroll. The rule prevents a plan from enrolling only the sickest employees. Priya’s plan is contributory; she needs at least fifteen of twenty eligible employees to join, not counting those covered by a spouse’s plan.

Noncontributory

Employer pays all; 100% participation

Contributory

Employees share cost; about 75% participation

05

Employer contribution

Carriers also require the employer to contribute a minimum share of the premium, commonly at least 50 percent of the employee-only cost. The contribution makes enrollment attractive to healthy employees, which keeps the risk pool balanced and the participation requirement reachable. Maryland’s small group market and the SHOP marketplace allow carriers to set reasonable participation and contribution requirements, applied uniformly, and to decline a small group that fails them, even though they may never decline for health. A small business tax credit may offset part of the employer’s share for qualifying employers that buy through SHOP.

50%

Common minimum employer share of employee premium

75%

Common minimum participation, contributory plan

Uniform

Requirements apply alike to all small groups

06

Why these rules exist

Every rule in this lesson fights the same enemy: adverse selection. Advertising rules keep employees from buying on false expectations. Jurisdiction rules make sure a state’s protections follow its residents. Group underwriting prices the whole group fairly without singling out sick employees. Participation and contribution requirements make sure the healthy join along with the sick. Theo tells Priya that meeting the participation rule is not bureaucracy; it is what keeps her premium affordable next year. Lesson 37 turns to the employees themselves: who is eligible, when they may enroll and how benefits coordinate.

More detail

Actively-at-work and eligible employees

Group underwriting typically counts only employees who are actively at work and meet the plan’s hours threshold; Maryland’s small group law defines full-time as 30 or more hours a week on average and lets the employer include part-time employees working at least 17.5 hours. Participation percentages are measured against these eligible employees.

Example: Priya’s six part-time workers at 20 hours a week may be included at her option; her 14 full-time workers must be offered coverage.

Large group flexibility

Large employers, generally those with more than 50 employees, may be experience rated and are not bound by the small group rating rules, though they must meet HIPAA nondiscrimination and ACA requirements. Carriers have more latitude to negotiate plan design and participation with them.

Example: A 300-employee logistics firm negotiates a custom plan design and an experience-rated premium; Priya’s bakery chooses from standard small group plans.
