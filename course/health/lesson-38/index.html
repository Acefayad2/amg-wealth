Explanations & examples

01

Switching carriers midyear

Priya moves her group to a new insurer on July 1. Without protection, her employees would face a second deductible in one year. A deductible carryover, or transfer of credit, provision counts expenses applied to the old plan’s deductible toward the new plan’s deductible for the rest of the year, and the same for coinsurance toward the out-of-pocket maximum. The no-loss no-gain rule requires the new carrier to cover employees as the old plan would have, including those currently disabled or hospitalized, so no one loses coverage because of the change and no one gains benefits they lacked before.

02

When group coverage ends

An employee’s coverage terminates when employment ends or hours fall below the eligible class, when the employee stops paying a required contribution, when the employer terminates the master policy, or when the employee enters the armed forces. A dependent’s coverage ends when the dependent no longer qualifies, for example a child turning 26 or a spouse after divorce. Each termination triggers rights: continuation, conversion or special enrollment elsewhere. Maryland requires the certificate to explain these rights, and employers must give notice promptly so that election deadlines can be met.

03

Military service and Medicare

An employee called to active duty may continue group coverage for up to 24 months under federal law and, on return, must be reinstated in the plan without a new waiting period or exclusion for conditions other than service-connected injuries, which the military covers. Group certificates also carry notices about Medicare eligibility, because turning 65 changes how the plan coordinates: for employers with 20 or more employees the group plan remains primary for active workers, and the employee should weigh Part B enrollment. Extension of benefits continues coverage for a totally disabled employee’s existing condition for a period after the master policy terminates, so a canceled policy does not abandon someone mid-treatment.

04

COBRA

Federal COBRA applies to employers with 20 or more employees. Termination for reasons other than gross misconduct, or reduced hours, allows 18 months of continuation; disability extends it to 29 months; death, divorce, Medicare entitlement or a child aging out gives dependents 36 months. The employer notifies the plan within 30 days, the plan notifies the beneficiary within 14 days, the beneficiary elects within 60 days and pays the first premium within 45 days of electing. The premium may be up to 102 percent of the full cost. Coverage ends early for nonpayment, new group coverage or the employer dropping all plans.

05

Maryland continuation

Maryland’s own continuation statutes reach group contracts of any size, including those too small for COBRA. After a loss of employment for reasons other than cause, an insured covered for at least three months may continue for up to 18 months, electing within 45 days, at no more than the combined employer and employee contribution plus an approved administrative fee. After the insured’s death, a spouse covered at least 30 days and dependent children may continue for 18 months on the same terms. After divorce, a former spouse covered 30 days may continue until remarriage or other coverage, with the insured paying through payroll deduction unless a court or agreement provides otherwise. Medicare entitlement ends continuation.

Job loss not for cause

18 months; 45-day election; 3 months prior coverage

Death of insured

18 months for spouse and children

Divorce

06

Conversion

Continuation keeps the group plan for a time; conversion replaces it with an individual policy. Under a conversion privilege, a person whose group coverage ends may buy an individual policy from the same insurer within 31 days without evidence of insurability. The individual policy may have different benefits and a higher premium. Maryland’s continuation statutes preserve conversion rights. Since the Affordable Care Act made individual coverage guaranteed issue, conversion matters less for medical plans, but an employee leaving Priya’s bakery still has three doors: Maryland continuation or COBRA, conversion with the carrier, or a special enrollment on Maryland Health Connection within 60 days.

More detail

COBRA and Maryland continuation side by side

COBRA requires 20 or more employees and a 60-day election; Maryland continuation applies to groups of any size with a 45-day election. Both allow 18 months after job loss. Maryland’s premium cap is the employer and employee contributions plus an approved administrative fee, while COBRA allows 102 percent of cost. A Maryland employee of a large employer may have rights under both; the more favorable terms apply.

Example: A 12-employee shop’s terminated worker relies on Maryland continuation; Priya’s 20-employee bakery triggers COBRA as well.

Termination statement after divorce

Maryland’s divorce continuation ends when the employer receives a termination statement signed by both former spouses, or by the insured with a sworn affidavit. The Commissioner prescribes the form, and receipt is conclusive evidence that the former spouse’s coverage ended.

Example: Six months after his divorce, an employee and his former wife sign the termination statement; her coverage under his plan ends on receipt.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Apply deductible carryover and no-loss no-gain rules when an employer changes carriers.
  • Identify events that terminate group coverage and the rules for military reinstatement, Medicare notification and extension of benefits.
  • Compare COBRA, Maryland continuation and conversion rights.
