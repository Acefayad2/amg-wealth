Explanations & examples

01

Part C: Medicare through a private plan

Medicare Advantage, Part C, lets Dennis receive his Medicare benefits through a private plan that Medicare pays a fixed monthly amount per member. To join he must have Parts A and B and live in the plan’s service area. The plan must cover everything Original Medicare covers, except hospice, which Original Medicare continues to pay, and most plans include Part D drug coverage and extras such as dental, vision, hearing and fitness benefits. Dennis keeps paying his Part B premium, and the plan may charge its own premium, often zero. The plan’s rules, networks and cost sharing replace Medicare’s.

Requirements

Parts A and B; live in the service area

Covers

All Part A and B services except hospice; usually Part D; extras

Pays

Medicare pays the plan a fixed amount per member

02

Advantage plan types and protections

Advantage plans come in the designs you know. HMOs require a primary care physician and referrals and cover nothing out of network except emergencies. PPOs pay more in network and less out. Private fee-for-service plans let members see any provider who accepts the plan’s terms. Special needs plans serve people with specific conditions, those in institutions or those also on Medicaid. Unlike Original Medicare, every Advantage plan has an annual out-of-pocket maximum for Part A and B services, a real protection. Plans may not deny enrollment for health conditions; ESRD patients may also enroll. Medicare publishes star ratings for quality.

HMO and PPO

Network rules as in commercial plans

PFFS and special needs plans

Any accepting provider; targeted populations

Out-of-pocket maximum

Required; Original Medicare has none

03

Advantage enrollment windows

Dennis may join an Advantage plan during his initial Medicare enrollment period. Each year the annual election period runs October 15 through December 7, when anyone may join, switch or drop an Advantage or Part D plan for January 1. A Medicare Advantage open enrollment period from January 1 through March 31 lets a current Advantage member switch once to another Advantage plan or return to Original Medicare with a Part D plan. Special enrollment periods follow moves, loss of other coverage, Medicaid changes and plan terminations. A member cannot hold a Medicare supplement policy while in an Advantage plan.

October 15 to December 7

Annual election period

January 1 to March 31

Advantage open enrollment: one switch

Special periods

Moves, plan exits, Medicaid changes

04

Part D: prescription drug coverage

Part D is optional outpatient drug coverage sold by private insurers, either as a stand-alone prescription drug plan paired with Original Medicare or built into an Advantage plan. Each plan has a formulary, a list of covered drugs arranged in tiers with different cost sharing, and may use prior authorization and step therapy. Enrollment windows match Part B’s initial period and the annual election period. The standard benefit has a deductible, 615 dollars in 2026, then cost sharing of about 25 percent until the member’s out-of-pocket spending reaches the annual cap, 2,100 dollars in 2026, after which covered drugs cost nothing for the rest of the year.

About 25%

$2,100

05

Part D penalties and help

Dennis can avoid a Part D late penalty by enrolling when he is first eligible or keeping drug coverage that is at least as good as Part D. If he goes 63 days in a row without that coverage after his initial enrollment period, a penalty can apply. For each full uncovered month, it is one percent of the national base beneficiary premium. He generally pays it as long as he has Part D. People who qualify for Extra Help do not owe this penalty. The Medicare Prescription Payment Plan is separate: it lets members spread their drug costs across the year.

Late penalty

After a 63-day gap: 1% of the national base beneficiary premium per full uncovered month

Creditable coverage

Avoids the penalty

Low-income subsidy

Payment plan

06

Two roads at 65

Dennis has two coherent choices. Road one: Original Medicare, a Medicare supplement policy to fill the deductibles and coinsurance, and a stand-alone Part D plan. He may see any provider who takes Medicare, nationwide, with predictable costs and higher monthly premiums. Road two: a Medicare Advantage plan, usually with drug coverage and extras, lower premiums, a network and referral rules, and an out-of-pocket maximum. He may switch between roads during election periods, but returning to a supplement later may require medical underwriting outside Maryland’s protected windows. Lesson 44 explains the supplement side; Lesson 45 explains Maryland’s protections.

Original Medicare plus Medigap plus Part D

Any Medicare provider; predictable costs; higher premiums

Medicare Advantage

Network and referrals; extras; lower premium; out-of-pocket cap

More detail

Hospice stays with Original Medicare

Even for Advantage members, hospice care is paid by Original Medicare Part A. The member may remain in the Advantage plan for other services during hospice.

Example: Gloria, in an Advantage HMO, elects hospice; Medicare Part A pays the hospice while the HMO continues her other coverage.

Trial right to return to a supplement

Federal rules give a person who joins an Advantage plan at 65 and leaves it within the first year, or who drops a Medigap policy to try Advantage for the first time and returns within 12 months, a guaranteed right to buy certain Medigap plans without underwriting.

Example: Dennis tries an Advantage plan at 65, dislikes the network and returns to Original Medicare in month nine; he can buy a supplement without health questions.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Explain how Medicare Advantage plans work, who may join and when.
  • Describe Part D coverage, its standard benefit design and the late enrollment penalty.
  • Distinguish Medicare Advantage from Original Medicare with a supplement.
