01

Filling the gaps

Original Medicare leaves Dennis with the Part A deductible each benefit period, 20 percent of Part B charges with no limit, possible excess charges and nothing for emergencies abroad. Medicare supplement insurance, or Medigap, is a private policy that pays those gaps. It works only with Original Medicare: Dennis must have Parts A and B, Medicare pays first and the supplement pays second, and he may not hold one while in a Medicare Advantage plan. Each policy covers one person, so Dennis and his wife need two. Medigap does not cover prescription drugs; a Part D plan does that.

02

Open enrollment

The most important date in Medigap is the six-month open enrollment period that begins the first month Dennis is 65 or older and enrolled in Part B. During those six months any insurer selling Medigap in Maryland must sell him any plan it offers, may not refuse him or charge more because of his health, and may not delay coverage of pre-existing conditions for more than six months, reduced by prior creditable coverage. After the window closes, insurers may underwrite, charge more or decline, except in guaranteed-issue situations. Lesson 45 adds Maryland’s extra protections, including a birthday window and rules for people under 65.

Guaranteed

03

Three ways to price

Insurers rate Medigap three ways. Attained-age rating charges a premium based on the insured’s current age, so the premium rises every year as Dennis gets older, on top of inflation increases; it is cheapest at 65 and most expensive later. Issue-age rating sets the premium by the age at purchase; it rises only for inflation, never because Dennis aged. Community rating charges everyone the same premium regardless of age, rising only for inflation; it is highest for the youngest buyers and fairest to the oldest. Theo shows Dennis the three price paths over twenty years, not just the first-year quote.

Attained age

Issue age

Community

Same for all ages; rises only for inflation

04

Standardized plans and core benefits

Federal law standardizes Medigap into lettered plans, A through N, so that a Plan G from one insurer is identical to a Plan G from another; only price and service differ. Every plan includes the core benefits: Part A hospital coinsurance for days 61 through 90 and the lifetime reserve days, plus 365 additional hospital days after Medicare benefits end; Part B coinsurance or copayments; the first three pints of blood; and Part A hospice coinsurance. Plan A offers only the core benefits. Every insurer that sells Medigap must offer Plan A.

Same letter, same benefits, any insurer

Core benefits

05

Additional benefits and high-deductible plans

The richer letters add benefits: the skilled nursing facility coinsurance for days 21 through 100, the Part A deductible, Part B excess charges, and emergency care during foreign travel at 80 percent up to a lifetime limit. Plans C and F also pay the Part B deductible, but federal law closed them to anyone who became eligible for Medicare on or after January 1, 2020, which made Plan G the most comprehensive plan for new enrollees. Plans K and L pay a percentage of some benefits in exchange for an out-of-pocket limit. High-deductible versions of Plans F and G pay nothing until the insured meets an annual deductible, indexed yearly, in exchange for a much lower premium.

06

Choosing a plan for Dennis

Dennis enrolls in Part B in March and shops in his six-month window. Plan G from three insurers covers the same things: everything but the Part B deductible. One insurer uses attained-age rating and is cheapest now; one uses issue-age and costs more now but will not rise with his age; one is community rated. He asks about high-deductible G as a lower-cost option. Because he is in open enrollment, none of them may ask about his health. Theo documents the comparison, explains that Plan G plus a Part D plan completes his coverage, and reminds him that the policy carries a 30-day right to return in Maryland.

More detail

Why Plans C and F were closed

Federal law prohibits selling newly eligible beneficiaries any Medigap plan that covers the Part B deductible, on the theory that first-dollar coverage encourages unnecessary use. People eligible before 2020 may keep or still buy Plans C and F.

Example: Gloria, on Medicare since 2015, keeps her Plan F; Dennis, newly eligible, chooses Plan G instead.

Waiver states

Massachusetts, Minnesota and Wisconsin standardize Medigap differently. Maryland uses the federal lettered plans, so the exam’s plan letters apply here.

Example: A Maryland Plan N has the same benefits as a Plan N sold in Virginia.
