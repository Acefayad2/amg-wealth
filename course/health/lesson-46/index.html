01

Working past 65

Dennis keeps his job at a 60-employee firm after turning 65. Under the Medicare secondary payer rules, when an active employee or spouse age 65 or older is covered by a group plan of an employer with 20 or more employees, the group plan pays first and Medicare pays second. If the employer has fewer than 20 employees, Medicare pays first and the group plan second. Retiree coverage is always secondary to Medicare. Because his employer is large, Dennis may delay Part B without penalty and enroll during the eight-month special enrollment period after he retires.

20 or more employees

Group plan primary; Medicare secondary

Fewer than 20

Medicare primary; group plan secondary

Retiree plan

Medicare primary

02

Disabled employees and kidney failure

Two more rules. A person under 65 who has Medicare because of disability and is covered by a group plan of an employer with 100 or more employees, through her own or a family member’s current employment, has the group plan primary and Medicare secondary; under 100, Medicare pays first. For end-stage renal disease, the group plan is primary for a 30-month coordination period beginning with Medicare eligibility, regardless of employer size, after which Medicare becomes primary. Employers may not offer incentives to drop group coverage in favor of Medicare or treat Medicare-eligible employees differently.

Disabled under 65

Group primary if employer has 100 or more employees

End-stage renal disease

Group primary for 30 months, any size; then Medicare

Employers

May not push Medicare-eligible workers off the plan

03

What Medicaid is

Medicaid is the joint federal and state program that pays for health care for people with limited income and resources. In Maryland it is called Medical Assistance and administered by the Department of Health, with federal rules and matching funds. Since the Affordable Care Act expansion, adults with household income up to 138 percent of the poverty level qualify on income alone; children, pregnant women and parents have higher thresholds, and the Maryland Children’s Health Program covers children in moderate-income families. Eligibility for long-term care coverage adds a resource test with a low asset limit and a five-year look-back on transfers of assets.

Who

Low income and resources; federal and state funded

Expansion adults

Income up to 138% of poverty

Long-term care eligibility

Asset test; 5-year look-back on transfers

04

What Medicaid pays

Medicaid covers mandatory benefits in every state: inpatient and outpatient hospital care, physician services, laboratory and x-ray, nursing facility care, home health, family planning, transportation to care and comprehensive screening and treatment for children. Maryland adds optional benefits including prescription drugs, dental for adults and home and community-based services that help people stay out of nursing homes. Medicaid is the payer of last resort: every other coverage pays first. People eligible for both Medicare and Medicaid are dual eligibles, and Medicaid or a Medicare Savings Program may pay their Medicare premiums and cost sharing.

Mandatory

Hospital, physician, lab, nursing facility, home health, children’s services

Maryland options

Drugs, adult dental, community-based services

Last resort

Pays after all other coverage

Dual eligibles

Medicaid helps with Medicare costs

05

Long-term care: who pays

Gloria wants to know who will pay if she needs to live in a nursing home. First, ask what kind of care she needs. In Original Medicare, Part A can cover up to 100 days of skilled nursing care in a benefit period if she meets the requirements. That usually includes a hospital stay of at least three days as an inpatient. She shares the cost after day 20. Help with bathing, dressing or eating alone is different. Medicare generally does not cover that ongoing help. Maryland Medicaid can help if she meets its financial and medical requirements. A private long-term care policy may also pay.

Medicare

Skilled care only; up to 100 days; no custodial

Medical insurance and Medigap

Exclude custodial care

Medicaid

Nursing facility care for eligible applicants

Long-term care insurance

Fills the gap

06

Three programs, one family

Dennis, 66 and working for a large employer, has his group plan primary and Medicare secondary; when he retires, Medicare becomes primary and a supplement fills the gaps. His brother, disabled at 58 and covered through a spouse’s 500-employee company, has the group plan primary. Gloria, 71 and on Medicare, can use Part A for a qualifying hospital stay and up to 100 days of skilled nursing care per benefit period. For a year of help with daily activities, she might use long-term care insurance, pay privately, or qualify for Medicaid. Lesson 47 builds that policy, and Lesson 48 adds Maryland’s rules for it.

Disabled, large employer

Retiree

Custodial care

More detail

Medicare Savings Programs

Maryland’s Medicare Savings Programs, including the Qualified Medicare Beneficiary program, use Medicaid funds to pay Part B premiums and, for QMB, Medicare deductibles and coinsurance for low-income beneficiaries. A QMB enrollee may not be billed Medicare cost sharing by providers.

Example: Gloria’s neighbor, with income near the poverty level, has her $202.90 Part B premium paid by the state.

Spend-down and the look-back

To qualify for Medicaid long-term care, an applicant’s countable assets must fall below the state limit; a spouse at home may keep a protected share. Assets given away within five years of applying can delay eligibility through a penalty period. Partnership long-term care policies, covered in Lesson 47, let a policyholder keep assets equal to the benefits the policy paid.

Example: A $100,000 gift to a grandchild three years before applying creates a Medicaid penalty period for Gloria.
