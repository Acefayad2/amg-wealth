01

Marketing and the consumer guide

Maryland adopted the NAIC long-term care model regulation in COMAR Title 31, Subtitle 14. Insurers must establish marketing procedures that prevent twisting, high-pressure tactics and cold lead advertising, and must assure that comparisons are fair and accurate. At or before application Theo must give Gloria the long-term care consumer guide, the shopper’s guide, and an outline of coverage that describes benefits, exclusions, renewability and premium increase history in a standard format, along with the disclosure titled Things You Should Know Before You Buy Long-Term Care Insurance. Advertising must be filed with the Commissioner.

Marketing standards

No twisting, pressure or cold leads; fair comparisons

Consumer guide and outline

Delivered at or before application

Disclosure

Things You Should Know Before You Buy

02

Suitability and the personal worksheet

Long-term care insurance is expensive and may be held for decades, so Maryland requires each insurer to adopt suitability standards and to use them in deciding whether to issue a policy. Producers must apply the same standards in marketing. Before or at application the applicant completes the long-term care insurance personal worksheet, covering premium as a share of income, assets and the applicant’s understanding that premiums may rise. The insurer reviews the worksheet; if the purchase appears unsuitable, it may decline or ask the applicant to confirm in writing that she wants the policy anyway. The insurer must also ask about existing coverage and Medicaid eligibility.

Personal worksheet

Income, assets, premium affordability

Insurer review

Suitability standards applied

Unsuitable

Decline, or the applicant confirms in writing

03

Return, replacement and renewal

Gloria has 30 days after delivery to return a long-term care policy for a full refund, and the policy must say so on its first page. If the policy replaces existing coverage, the producer gives a replacement notice, the insurer credits time served and waives pre-existing condition periods for benefits the old policy covered, and the producer is paid only a renewal commission on the replacement. Every individual long-term care policy must be guaranteed renewable or noncancelable; a conditionally renewable or optionally renewable long-term care policy is not permitted. Group policies must offer continuation or conversion when a member leaves the group.

30 days

Right to return, on the first page

Replacement

Notice; credit for time served; renewal commission only

Renewal

Guaranteed renewable or noncancelable only

Group

Continuation or conversion

04

Disclosure, incontestability and lapse protection

Insurers must disclose that premiums may increase and must report their rate increase history. Incontestability is graduated: within six months the insurer may rescind for a material misrepresentation; between six months and two years only if the misrepresentation relates to the condition claimed; after two years only if the applicant knowingly and intentionally misrepresented relevant health facts. Benefits already paid are not recovered. To prevent unintentional lapse, the applicant may name a third party to receive lapse notices, the insurer must give 30 days’ notice before terminating for nonpayment, and a policy that lapsed because of cognitive or functional impairment may be reinstated within five months.

6 months

Rescission for material misrepresentation

6 months to 2 years

Only if related to the claimed condition

After 2 years

Only knowing and intentional misrepresentation

Lapse

Third-party notice; 30 days; reinstatement within 5 months

05

Inflation protection, pre-existing conditions, nonforfeiture and triggers

Every insurer must offer inflation protection of at least 5 percent compounded annually and may issue a policy without it only when the applicant rejects it in writing. A pre-existing condition may be defined no more strictly than one treated in the six months before coverage, and may be excluded for no more than six months after the effective date. Insurers must offer a nonforfeiture benefit, usually a shortened benefit period, and must provide a contingent nonforfeiture benefit when a substantial premium increase causes lapse. Benefit triggers may be no more restrictive than two of six activities of daily living or cognitive impairment, and no policy may require a prior hospital stay or condition home care on prior facility care.

Inflation protection

5% compound offered; written rejection

Pre-existing

6-month look-back; 6-month exclusion maximum

Nonforfeiture

Offered; contingent benefit on large rate increases

Triggers

Two of six ADLs or cognitive impairment; no prior hospitalization

06

The Maryland tax credit

Maryland rewards buying long-term care coverage with an income tax credit. Under Tax-General Section 10-718, an individual who pays premiums for a qualified long-term care policy covering herself, her spouse, a parent or stepparent, or a child or stepchild who is a Maryland resident may claim 100 percent of the eligible premiums paid that year, up to 500 dollars per insured. The credit is annual, may be claimed by only one taxpayer per insured, cannot exceed the state tax after other credits and cannot be carried forward. It is unavailable if the insured held long-term care insurance before July 1, 2000 or the credit was claimed for that insured before.

Credit

100% of eligible premiums

Cap

$500 per insured per year

Who

Self, spouse, parent, child; Maryland resident insured

Limits

One taxpayer; no carryover; not if covered before July 1, 2000

More detail

Contingent nonforfeiture

If an insurer raises premiums by more than a threshold percentage that depends on the insured’s age at issue, and the policyholder lets the policy lapse, Maryland requires a contingent nonforfeiture benefit: a paid-up policy with a shortened benefit period equal to the premiums paid, even if the insured declined the nonforfeiture option at purchase.

Example: After a 60 percent rate increase, Gloria stops paying; she keeps a paid-up benefit equal to the premiums she paid over the years.

Applications and older applicants

Maryland requires long-term care applications to ask clear, unambiguous health questions and to have the applicant list prescribed medications; a listed medication the insurer should have recognized bars rescission for that condition. Applicants 80 and older must have a physical examination, a functional assessment or a review of medical records before issue.

Example: Gloria, 71, answers the health questions and lists her blood pressure medication; her 82-year-old neighbor also completes a functional assessment.

Suitability is not underwriting

Underwriting asks whether the insurer is willing to accept the risk. Suitability asks whether the purchase makes sense for the buyer given her income and assets. A healthy applicant who can barely afford the premium may pass underwriting and fail suitability.

Example: An 80-year-old with $900 of monthly income is insurable but unsuitable for a $400 monthly premium; the insurer asks her to confirm in writing or declines.
