Explanations & examples

01

Misrepresentation and false advertising

Misrepresentation is giving a false or misleading account of a policy: its benefits, exclusions, premiums, dividends or the insurer’s financial condition. False advertising is the same deception in a brochure, website, seminar or ad. Telling Priya that a plan covers all prescriptions when it has a formulary, or calling a hospital indemnity policy health insurance, misrepresents. Omitting a material limitation can mislead as surely as a false statement. Maryland treats both as unfair trade practices under Section 27-202, with penalties, restitution and license discipline. The cure is the same standard from Lesson 10: say what the policy does, what it does not, and who stands behind it.

Misrepresentation

False or misleading account of a policy or insurer

False advertising

The same deception in marketing materials

Includes

Material omissions, not only false statements

02

Defamation and false entries

Competition is lawful; lying about competitors is not. Defamation under Section 27-204 is making or circulating a false or maliciously critical statement about an insurer’s financial condition that is calculated to injure its business. Theo may point out that a rival plan has a narrower network; he may not claim the rival is insolvent without basis. Section 27-205 prohibits knowingly false statements about an insurer’s financial condition and false or deceptive entries in insurer records, including altering records to mislead an examiner. Fabricating a client’s signature on an application or backdating a receipt falls here as well as under fraud.

Lawful

Accurate comparison of documented features

Defamation

False or malicious statements about an insurer’s finances

False entries

Deceptive records, forged signatures, altered documents

03

Rebating

A rebate is anything of value given to induce a purchase that is not specified in the policy: returning part of the commission, paying the first premium, giving a gift card after the sale. Section 27-209 prohibits rebating and inducements. Maryland allows educational materials, promotional materials and merchandise costing no more than 50 dollars, but receipt may not be conditioned on buying insurance. A 20 dollar notebook handed to everyone at a seminar is lawful; the same notebook promised only to those who enroll is a rebate. Both parts of the exception, the dollar limit and the no-condition rule, must be met. Rebating is a violation for the producer and for a client who knowingly accepts it.

Rebate

Value not in the policy, given to induce a sale

Exception

Materials or merchandise up to $50

Condition

Never tied to a purchase

04

Twisting

Twisting is inducing a policyholder to lapse, surrender, replace, convert or retain a policy through misrepresentation or an incomplete comparison. Section 27-213 covers retention as well as replacement: telling Rosa her current insurer is failing to make her switch is twisting, and so is falsely telling her the new plan is worse to keep her from switching. An honest, complete comparison that leads to a replacement is lawful, as Lesson 14 explained. The difference lies in the accuracy and completeness of what the producer says. Medigap and long-term care replacement rules add their own notices on top of the general prohibition.

Statement

Misleading or incomplete comparison

Purpose

Induce lapse, replacement or retention

Result

Twisting, regardless of whether the client switched

05

Referrals and boycott

Paying someone for referrals is lawful only within limits. A fee to a person who does not sell, solicit or negotiate may be paid only if it is not conditioned on a sale and does not operate as a rebate to the buyer; a payment that depends on the referred customer buying is commission sharing with an unlicensed person. Describing active sales work as a referral does not change its nature. Maryland also prohibits boycott, coercion and intimidation: agreeing with others to refuse to deal with an insurer, or pressuring a lender’s customers to buy insurance through a particular producer as a condition of credit, are unfair trade practices.

Referral fee

Lawful only if not tied to a sale

Disguised commission

Payment conditioned on purchase to an unlicensed person

Boycott, coercion, intimidation

Prohibited pressure on insurers or customers

06

Name the practice

When a question describes conduct, match it to the practice. A false statement about a policy to make a sale: misrepresentation. The same statement in a mailer: false advertising. A false claim that an insurer is broke: defamation. A forged signature or altered record: false entry. Cash or a gift tied to buying: rebating. A misleading comparison to move or keep a policyholder: twisting. A payment to an unlicensed friend that depends on the sale: unlawful commission sharing dressed as a referral. Each carries administrative penalties, restitution and possible license action, and several are also crimes.

Misrepresentation

False account to sell

Defamation and false entries

Attacking insurers; falsifying records

Rebating

Value tied to a purchase

Twisting

Misleading comparison to replace or retain

More detail

Insurer products and services are not rebates

Section 27–209 separately allows insurers to offer qualifying value-added products or services related to the coverage, such as wellness programs or loss-control services, under stated conditions. These are insurer offerings disclosed to all similarly situated insureds, not producer cash inducements.

Example: A carrier’s free nurse hotline for all members is permitted; Theo’s personal offer to pay a client’s first premium is not.

Blank signed forms

Asking a client to sign a blank or incomplete application so the producer can fill it in later is a false-entry and accuracy problem and a frequent source of complaints. Applications are completed with the client, corrections are initialed and the insurer’s correction process is used for later changes.

Example: Theo never leaves Rosa’s kitchen with a signed blank form; every answer is recorded before she signs.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Identify misrepresentation and false advertising in health insurance sales.
  • Distinguish defamation and false statements or entries from legitimate competition.
  • Apply Maryland’s rebating, twisting and referral rules, including the $50 promotional exception.
