01

The master rule

Rosa asks whether her disability benefits will be taxed. The answer to almost every health insurance tax question is the same: follow the premium. If the premium was paid with after-tax dollars and never deducted, the benefit is tax-free. If the premium was deducted, excluded from income or paid by an employer, the benefit is taxable, except where Congress made a specific exception, as it did for medical expense reimbursements. Learn that rule, then learn the exceptions. For individually owned coverage the premium is almost always after-tax, so benefits are almost always tax-free; the question is only whether the premium itself can be deducted.

Premium after tax, not deducted

Benefit tax-free

Premium deducted or employer-paid

Benefit taxable, unless an exception applies

Exception

Medical expense reimbursements stay tax-free

02

Individual disability income

Rosa pays her own disability premium. She may not deduct it; disability income insurance is not a medical expense for tax purposes, because it replaces income rather than paying for care. In exchange, every dollar of benefit she receives while disabled is free of federal income tax. That is why insurers cap benefits at 60 to 70 percent of gross income: tax-free benefits at that level approach her take-home pay. If Rosa later deducts the premium as a business expense, which the law does not permit for her own coverage, the benefits would become taxable. Keep the premium after-tax and the benefits stay clean.

Premium

Not deductible

Benefits

Tax-free

03

Individual medical expense

Marcus buys an individual medical plan. Its premiums are a medical expense, deductible only if he itemizes and only to the extent his total unreimbursed medical expenses, including premiums, exceed 7.5 percent of adjusted gross income. Most people never clear that floor, so most individual medical premiums produce no deduction. Benefits are different: amounts the plan pays to doctors and hospitals, or reimburses to Marcus for medical care, are never taxable income, even in the rare case where he deducted the premium. Premiums paid through the Exchange with a premium tax credit are reconciled on the return; the credit itself is not income.

Medical expense, deductible above 7.5% of AGI if itemizing

Benefits

Premium tax credit

04

Individual long-term care

Gloria’s tax-qualified long-term care premium is also a medical expense, but with a twist: only an age-based portion counts, indexed each year and rising from a few hundred dollars for people under 40 to several thousand for those over 70, and the 7.5 percent floor still applies. Benefits from a qualified policy are tax-free when they reimburse actual costs, and indemnity benefits are tax-free up to a daily per diem limit, indexed yearly, with amounts above it taxable unless they match actual expenses. Maryland adds its own credit of up to 500 dollars per insured, from Lesson 48. Nonqualified policies carry no assured federal treatment.

Premium

Medical expense up to an age-based limit; 7.5% floor

Benefits

Tax-free: actual costs, or indemnity up to the daily cap

Maryland credit

Up to $500 per insured

05

HSAs and HRAs

A health savings account is the most tax-favored of the three accounts. Rosa’s contributions are deductible, or excluded from pay if made through payroll; earnings grow untaxed; withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free; and the balance is hers for life. Nonqualified withdrawals are taxed and, before 65, penalized 20 percent. A health reimbursement arrangement is funded only by the employer, whose contributions are deductible business expenses and are not income to the employee; reimbursements for medical expenses are tax-free. The employee contributes nothing, so there is no deduction to claim and nothing to penalize.

HSA

Deductible in; tax-free growth; tax-free for medical; 20% penalty before 65 otherwise

HRA

Employer-funded; deductible to employer; tax-free to employee

06

FSAs and high-deductible plans

Marcus’s employer offers a health flexible spending account. He elects to reduce his salary by up to the annual limit, 3,400 dollars for 2026, and that money escapes income and payroll taxes; reimbursements of medical, dental and vision expenses are tax-free. The catch is use-it-or-lose-it: unused funds are forfeited at year end unless the plan allows a limited carryover or a grace period. A high-deductible health plan is simply a medical plan whose premiums follow the medical expense rules above; its tax significance is that it unlocks HSA eligibility. Compare the three: the HSA belongs to the individual, the HRA to the employer, and the FSA to the plan year.

FSA

Pre-tax salary reduction; $3,400 in 2026; use it or lose it

HSA

Individual’s account; portable

HRA

Employer’s promise

HDHP

Medical plan that unlocks the HSA

More detail

Indexed numbers

The 7.5 percent floor is fixed in current law, but the long-term care premium limits, the per diem benefit cap, the FSA limit and the HSA limits are indexed annually. Learn the structure and confirm the year’s figures from the IRS before advising a client.

Example: Rosa’s $4,400 HSA limit for 2026 will rise again in 2027; the deductibility of her contribution will not change.

Why FSAs and HSAs rarely mix

A general purpose health FSA pays medical expenses before the HDHP deductible is met, which makes the employee ineligible to contribute to an HSA. Employers offering HDHPs usually convert their FSA to a limited-purpose plan for dental and vision so employees can hold both.

Example: Marcus keeps a limited-purpose FSA for the children’s braces while contributing to his family HSA.
