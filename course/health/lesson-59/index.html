Case one: the hospital bill

Marcus’s PPO has a 1,500 dollar deductible, 80 percent in-network coinsurance, 60 percent out of network and a 5,000 dollar out-of-pocket maximum. He is admitted to an in-network hospital with allowed charges of 28,000 dollars. Work it in order. Deductible first: 1,500. Coinsurance second: 20 percent of the remaining 26,500 is 5,300. Cap last: his total of 6,800 exceeds the 5,000 maximum, so he pays 5,000 and the plan pays 23,000. If the same stay had been out of network, the lower benefit level and any balance bill would apply, and the surprise billing rules might protect him only for emergency care.

02

Case two: the missed premium

Rosa pays her disability premium quarterly. The premium due April 1 is not paid. Her policy allows the 31-day grace period that applies to quarterly premiums, so she is covered through May 1. On April 20 she is injured. The claim is covered, less the overdue premium. She must give notice within 20 days of the loss, the insurer must send claim forms within 15 days, and she must furnish proof of loss within 90 days. Had the injury come on May 15 after a lapse, she would need reinstatement, and a sickness beginning within ten days of reinstatement would not be covered.

April 1

Premium due; 31-day grace for quarterly

April 20

Injury within grace; covered

20, 15, 90

Notice, forms, proof

Case three: the partial return

Rosa’s policy pays 3,600 dollars a month after a 90-day elimination period, with a residual benefit and a social insurance supplement of 1,200 dollars. She is totally disabled on June 1. Benefits accrue from August 30 and the first check arrives at the end of September. In month ten Social Security approves her for 1,500 dollars a month, so the supplement stops. In month fourteen she returns half time with a 40 percent income loss; her residual benefit is 40 percent of 3,600, which is 1,440, and her waiver of premium continues as long as she remains disabled under the policy.

Elimination ends

Until Social Security pays

$1,440

04

Case four: the layoff

Elena’s coworker at a 12-employee clinic is laid off on March 31 after two years of coverage. COBRA does not apply, because the employer has fewer than 20 employees. Maryland continuation does: she had more than three months of coverage and lost her job for a reason other than cause, so she may elect within 45 days to continue the group plan for up to 18 months at the employer-plus-employee premium plus an approved fee. She may also convert to an individual policy within 31 days, or use a 60-day special enrollment period on Maryland Health Connection, where a premium tax credit may make a marketplace plan cheaper than continuation.

COBRA

Does not apply under 20 employees

Maryland continuation

45 days to elect; 18 months

Alternatives

Conversion within 31 days; Exchange within 60 days

05

Case five: two plans, one child

Marcus, born in September, and Elena, born in March, both carry family coverage. Their son’s allowed charges are 6,000 dollars. Elena’s plan is primary under the birthday rule and pays 4,200 after its deductible and coinsurance. Marcus’s plan is secondary and would have paid 4,800 alone, so it pays the 1,800 balance and the family owes nothing. In July Marcus’s employer changes carriers; a deductible carryover credits what he already paid, and no-loss no-gain keeps his son’s ongoing orthopedic treatment covered. In November Elena’s hours drop below the eligible class, triggering her own continuation rights.

Primary

Elena’s plan, earlier birthday: $4,200

Secondary

Marcus’s plan pays the $1,800 balance

Carrier change

Carryover; no-loss no-gain

Case six: choosing the plan

Three clients, three designs. Priya’s younger employees want the lowest premium and accept a network and referrals: an HMO. Marcus wants to keep a specialist outside any network and will pay more: a PPO. Rosa wants HMO pricing most of the time with the option to go outside when it matters: a point-of-service plan. For each, the questions are the same. Is a referral required? Is there any out-of-network benefit? Are copayments or coinsurance used? Add the Lesson 19 arithmetic and the Lesson 23 coordination rules, and most plan questions on the exam become a matter of reading carefully and working in order.

HMO

Lowest cost; referrals; network only

PPO

Choice; two benefit levels

POS

Gatekeeper inside; coverage outside

Method

Rule first, then numbers

More detail

Order of operations for cost sharing

Always apply the deductible, then coinsurance on the remainder, then compare the insured’s total to the out-of-pocket maximum. Copayments usually bypass the deductible but count toward the maximum. Premiums never count.

Example: A $500 copayment-based plan with a $3,000 maximum: the insured pays copayments until they total $3,000, then nothing more for covered in-network care.

Reading a group termination question

Identify the employer size, the reason coverage ended, how long the person was covered and the state. Then pick the right door: COBRA, Maryland continuation, conversion or Exchange special enrollment, remembering that more than one may be open at once.

Example: A 25-employee firm’s terminated worker has both COBRA and Maryland continuation rights; the more favorable terms apply.
