01

Case one: the first application

Theo passes the exam, pays the 54 dollar fee and receives his license in January. In February a carrier he has never worked with accepts a health application he solicited; it must appoint him or reject the application within 30 days, and it appoints him, sending written documentation within 30 days. In April he moves and updates his address within 30 days. In October he is charged with a misdemeanor unrelated to driving and reports it within 30 days of his first court appearance, attaching the charging document. Every clock in his first year is 30 days except the one he must not confuse: continuing education, due 15 days before his license expires.

Before expiration: 24 hours including 3 ethics

02

Case two: money and favors

In March a client pays a 1,200 dollar annual premium in cash. Theo deposits it in AMG’s premium account, never the operating account, and remits it to the carrier. A different carrier pays no commission on a small plan, so Theo charges a placement fee; the law caps it at 15 percent of the premium, 180 dollars on 1,200. A prospect hesitates, and Theo is tempted to offer to pay her first month; that would be rebating. Instead he gives her the same 20 dollar planning guide he gives everyone, which is lawful because it costs under 50 dollars and is not conditioned on buying.

Premium

Fiduciary funds in the premium account

Fee

15% cap when no commission: $180 on $1,200

Rebate

Paying the premium is prohibited

Promotional item

Under $50, not conditioned on purchase

03

Case three: the comparison

A competitor’s client asks Theo to compare plans. Theo lays out both plans accurately, including the competitor’s broader network and his own plan’s lower premium, and documents the comparison. That is a fair presentation, and if the client switches, a lawful replacement. He does not tell her the competitor is in financial trouble, which would be defamation if false, and he does not hide his plan’s referral requirement, which would make the comparison twisting. He also knows that a false claim in a mailer would be false advertising, for which his insurer and he would both answer.

Lawful

Accurate, complete, documented comparison

Defamation

False statements about an insurer’s finances

Twisting

Hiding a material term to induce replacement

04

Case four: Priya’s family plan

Priya’s bakery, with 18 average employees, is a small employer and enjoys guaranteed availability and community rating. One baker’s 24-year-old married son is covered: Maryland requires coverage of unmarried children to 25, but the federal rule to 26 regardless of marriage governs her plan. Another baker has a child on January 10; the baby is covered from birth, and because the plan charges per child, notice and premium are due within 31 days. A third baker is ordered by a court to cover a child who lives with the other parent; the carrier enrolls the child at once, outside any enrollment period.

Small employer

Average of not more than 50; guaranteed issue; community rated

Dependents

Maryland 25; federal 26 controls

Newborn

From birth; 31-day notice when premium is charged

Support order

Enroll regardless of enrollment periods

05

Case five: Dennis and Gloria

Dennis enrolls in Part B in March at 65. His six-month Medigap open enrollment runs through August; the Plan G policy Theo delivers on April 10 carries a 30-day return right through May 10. Next year, in the 30 days after his birthday, he may switch to an equal or lesser plan without underwriting under Maryland’s birthday rule. Gloria, 71, considers long-term care insurance. Theo gives her the consumer guide, the outline of coverage and the personal worksheet, the insurer offers 5 percent compound inflation protection and a nonforfeiture benefit, and her 3,000 dollar premium earns a 500 dollar Maryland tax credit.

6 months

Medigap open enrollment from Part B

30 days

Return right; birthday rule window

LTC sale

Guide, outline, worksheet; inflation and nonforfeiture offers

$500

Maryland LTC credit per insured per year

06

Case six: the complaint

In December a former client complains to the Administration that Theo misdescribed a limited policy. The Commissioner sends notice of a proposed penalty; Theo demands a hearing in writing, and it is held within 30 days. His documented sales presentation, outline of coverage and delivery receipt show what he said and gave the client. The complaint is dismissed. Had the facts been otherwise, the penalty could have run from 100 to 5,000 dollars per violation with restitution, and a revocation would have left him 30 days to seek judicial review. Records, candor and calendars are the whole of Maryland compliance.

Notice

Proposed action and grounds

Hearing

Demanded in writing; held within 30 days

Penalty range

$100 to $5,000 per violation

Defense

Documentation of what was said and delivered

More detail

Thirty days is not one rule

Many Maryland deadlines are 30 days, but they attach to different events: an insurer’s appointment duties, a producer’s address change, criminal and administrative reports, the hearing period, judicial review and the Medigap return right. Name the event before naming the clock, and remember the exceptions: 15 days for continuing education, 45 days for Maryland continuation elections, 31 days for newborn notice.

Example: Theo’s exam question says a producer changed addresses; the answer is 30 days from the move, not from renewal.

When to call the Administration

Producers may contact the Maryland Insurance Administration to verify an insurer’s authorization, check their own license status and appointments, ask about a regulation or report suspected fraud. Using the regulator as a resource before a problem is part of the job.

Example: Before placing business with an unfamiliar carrier, Theo checks its certificate of authority on the Administration’s website.
