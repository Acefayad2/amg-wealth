Explanations & examples

01

Start with the action

Marcus now understands pooling. His next question is practical: does buying life insurance remove the risk of death? No. It transfers specified financial consequences under a contract. To classify a risk-management action, ask what changes. Does the person avoid an exposure, keep the financial responsibility, share it, reduce the chance or size of loss, or transfer specified costs? One family can use several methods together. The name of the action matters less than what that action actually accomplishes.

02

Avoid, reduce or retain

Consider three decisions. Marcus chooses not to take part in recreational rock climbing. He avoids that particular exposure, although he has not avoided every risk in life. He installs a handrail on a slippery stairway. That reduces the likelihood of a fall; it does not guarantee safety. He also decides to pay for small, affordable losses from his own savings. That is retention. Retention may be intentional or result from simply having no insurance. Savings finance a loss; they do not prevent it.

03

Share or transfer

Sharing spreads a financial burden among participants. Pooling illustrates that idea across a group. Transfer moves a specified financial obligation to another party, often through insurance. Marcus buys a policy that pays a stated benefit for a covered death. His family still experiences the loss of a person, while the insurer has the contractual payment obligation. Insurance does not transfer every possible consequence, and exclusions or limits still matter. Sharing and transfer can both appear in the same insurance arrangement at different levels.

Spread a burden across participants

Transfer

Another party accepts a defined obligation

04

What makes a risk insurable?

Insurance works best with many similar exposure units and losses that are accidental from the insured's perspective, definite enough to identify, and measurable in financial terms. The probability and cost must be reasonably estimable, and the premium economically feasible. Large, correlated catastrophes can strain a pool, so insurers manage concentration. These are useful characteristics, not a promise that every risk meeting a checklist will be accepted. Pure risk offers loss or no loss. Speculative risk also offers possible gain, such as buying stock hoping its price rises.

05

The insurer can transfer risk too

Now follow a different contract. An insurer accepts Marcus's policy, then arranges for a reinsurer to take part of its insurance risk. This is reinsurance. The original insurer is the ceding insurer; the other company is the reinsurer. Unless a separate legal arrangement changes the policy obligation, Marcus's insurer still owes the policy benefit. He does not normally file part of his claim with the reinsurer. Reinsurance can help manage large individual exposures, accumulated losses, and the insurer's capacity to write business.

06

Use the distinction

Imagine a family maintains safe stairs, holds an emergency fund, and purchases life insurance. The stairs reduce a hazard. The fund supports retained costs. The policy transfers covered financial risk. If the insurer buys reinsurance, that creates another transfer between insurers. On a question, locate the parties and the event first. Then identify the action's effect. Do not call every sensible precaution avoidance, and do not assume every transfer erases the underlying danger. Answer the lesson questions using those distinctions.

More detail

Insurability is not underwriting approval

A risk can have many desirable insurance characteristics and still fall outside a particular insurer’s eligibility rules or capacity. A single policy transfers specified financial risk; it does not guarantee payment outside its coverage.

Example: A stock-market investment can rise or fall. Ordinary life insurance is not a guarantee of that investment’s profit.

Sharing and reinsurance use different relationships

Sharing describes distribution of a burden among participants. Reinsurance is a contract under which one insurer transfers insurance risk to another. The policyholder’s direct contract is ordinarily with the original insurer.

Example: A reinsurer agrees to reimburse an insurer for part of covered claims. That agreement does not ordinarily require the family to submit a second death claim to the reinsurer.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Distinguish five methods of handling risk.
  • Recognize characteristics that make a risk insurable.
  • Explain reinsurance without changing the policyholder’s insurer.
