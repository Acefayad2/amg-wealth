Explanations & examples

01

Four roles can involve different people

Marcus applies for life insurance on himself, pays the premium and names his sister Aisha as beneficiary. Marcus is the insured because the contract covers his life. He is also the policyowner, with the rights granted by the contract, and the premium payer. Aisha is the designated recipient of the death benefit under the policy's terms. In another arrangement, these roles can belong to different people. Always identify each role from the facts. Paying a premium alone does not prove that the payer owns the contract or controls its beneficiary designation.

02

Insurance on your own life

Maryland permits an individual with competent legal capacity to obtain coverage on that individual's own life for the benefit of any person. Marcus can therefore make a genuine own-life purchase and name the beneficiary he chooses, subject to the contract and applicable law. Do not confuse this with an unrelated investor causing a policy to be purchased on Marcus for the investor's benefit. The actual procurement matters. A signature used to disguise a stranger's scheme does not establish that the transaction was genuinely Marcus's own decision and purchase.

03

Family relationships can establish interest

Now Elena wants to obtain coverage on her spouse. Maryland recognizes a substantial interest based on love and affection for people closely related by blood or law. This is not a requirement that every spouse prove a specific unpaid debt. Other relationships need their own analysis. Friendship alone does not automatically establish the statutory family interest. The law also addresses prospective adoption with defined events and required conditions. Read the relationship carefully, then apply the appropriate rule. A producer should not replace the legal test with assumptions about a person's name, appearance or community.

04

Economic interest protects continued life

A small firm depends on Daniel's specialized work. His death would create a real economic loss through recruiting, training and interrupted operations. A lawful and substantial economic interest in his continued life can support an insurable interest, subject to the applicable requirements. Contrast an unrelated stranger who profits only if Daniel dies. An interest created only by death, or increased in value by death, is not the required economic interest. Insurance should protect a legitimate relationship or exposure. It should not create a wager on a stranger's death.

05

Test the interest at the right time

For a person procuring coverage on another individual, Maryland's general rule permits the benefits to be payable to the insured, the insured's personal representative or a person with an insurable interest when the contract is made. Focus on that starting point. Do not move the test automatically to the claim date. Later ownership changes, assignments and beneficiary questions require their own rules. Likewise, a changed family relationship can raise separate legal issues. Establishing insurable interest when the policy begins is one question; determining who receives a later benefit is another.

06

Consent is a separate checkpoint

Insurable interest does not by itself answer every consent question. For an ordinary individual contract within Maryland's general rule, the insured must have competent legal capacity and apply for or consent in writing to the contract. The statute has specific exceptions involving spouses, minors, family policies and a person unable to consent. Group life is outside that subsection. Apply the actual exception rather than announcing that written consent is either always required or never required. Before submitting a file, confirm the roles, the interest, the required application and the insurer's documentation process.

More detail

Read the Maryland consent exceptions precisely

§12–202 permits a spouse to put coverage into effect on the other spouse; it also addresses coverage on a minor by a person with an insurable interest or on whom the minor depends, family applications signed by specified family members, and a written application by a person with insurable interest for someone lacking legal capacity. These exceptions do not authorize forged signatures or ignoring applicable insurer procedures.

Example: A question expressly invoking the spouse exception should not be answered using an absolute rule that every insured personally signs every individual application.

A trust does not automatically solve an interest problem

§12–201 includes a trustee rule involving the insured’s relationship to the grantor and proceeds primarily benefiting trust beneficiaries with insurable interest. It also states ownership-related conditions for partnerships and limited liability companies. Use the actual conditions rather than treating a legal entity as automatic permission.

Example: Moving a stranger-originated plan into a trust does not itself demonstrate compliance.

Business and charity provisions have their own facts

The statute recognizes specified buy-sell parties’ interests for the agreement’s purpose. It separately permits qualified charitable institutions and qualifying trusts to procure coverage when the institution or trust is irrevocably designated and the insured or legal guardian signs the application. Corporate employee provisions have additional conditions, and an employer with the specified interest may not retaliate for refusal to consent.

Example: A charity’s qualification, irrevocable designation and signed application matter; simply calling the beneficiary a charity is not the whole rule.

Timing does not settle every later beneficiary dispute

Insurable interest at contract formation is distinct from the legal effects of divorce, an assignment, an irrevocable beneficiary or an estate designation. Those issues receive separate lessons.

Example: A question about whether the original policy had a valid insurable interest should not be answered by assuming an unspecified later divorce automatically voids the original contract.

Authority follows the role and the contract

The owner’s rights may include beneficiary changes, assignments and policy elections, subject to restrictions such as an irrevocable beneficiary or an assignment. The insured’s life is covered; that fact alone does not give an insured who is not the owner every ownership right.

Example: An employer paying a premium on an employee-owned executive-bonus policy does not become owner merely by making that payment.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Identify the owner, insured, premium payer and beneficiary.
  • Recognize family and economic insurable interests when a contract is made.
  • Distinguish an own-life purchase from procurement on another person.
