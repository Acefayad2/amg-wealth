Explanations & examples

01

Identify the loss the business faces

Daniel and Elena own a repair company. They face several different problems if someone dies. An owner's family may inherit a business interest without wanting to operate the company. Losing a key manager may interrupt revenue and require a costly replacement. The company may also want to reward and retain a valuable employee. Life insurance can play a role in each situation, but the owner, beneficiary and intended use of proceeds differ. Start with the business objective. Then trace who owns the policy, whose life is insured and who receives the money.

Ownership transition

Buy the deceased owner’s interest

Operating loss

Replace skills and stabilize operations

Employee reward

Support employee-owned protection

02

A buy-sell agreement sets the obligation

Daniel and Elena want the surviving owner to buy the deceased owner's business interest at an agreed value or under an agreed valuation method. The buy-sell agreement creates the purchase framework. Life insurance can supply money for the death-triggered purchase. The agreement and the policy are separate documents. A policy alone does not decide the share price, force a transfer or resolve every ownership issue. The business must coordinate the legal agreement, valuation, ownership structure and insurance amount. Review the arrangement when the business value or the owners change.

Agreement

Who buys, when and on what terms

Insurance

Funding for the covered death event

Purchase

Transfer the interest under the agreement

03

Cross-purchase: owners buy from the estate

In a simple two-owner cross-purchase arrangement, Elena owns and is beneficiary of a policy on Daniel. Daniel owns and is beneficiary of a policy on Elena. If Daniel dies and the policy pays, Elena receives the proceeds. She uses the money under the buy-sell agreement to purchase Daniel's interest from his estate or other lawful successor. The surviving owner is the buyer in this example. The family receives the purchase price for the interest. Do not assume that the deceased owner's family is also the beneficiary of the funding policy.

Elena’s policy

Elena owns coverage on Daniel

Daniel’s death

Covered proceeds go to Elena

Ownership purchase

Elena pays the lawful successor

04

Entity-purchase: the business buys

Change the structure. The repair company owns and is beneficiary of policies on its owners. If Daniel dies, the company receives the covered proceeds and uses them under the agreement to buy back his interest. This is an entity-purchase arrangement, also called a stock-redemption arrangement in a corporate setting. The business, rather than Elena individually, makes the purchase. Do not choose between structures from a slogan about taxes. Their legal, valuation and tax consequences differ. The learning task is to follow the ownership and payment flow and recognize the arrangement described.

Company-owned policy

The company insures Daniel

Covered proceeds

Paid to the company

Entity purchase

The company buys back his interest

05

Key-person insurance addresses an operating loss

Now suppose the company depends on Marcus, a specialist whose relationships and technical skills support important contracts. The company buys life coverage on Marcus to protect its own lawful economic interest, with the required application and consent process. The company generally owns the policy, pays the premiums and receives the covered death benefit. It may use the funds for recruiting, training, lost revenue or debt obligations. This does not automatically buy an ownership interest or pay a personal family benefit. Key-person coverage protects the business against its loss from a valuable person's death.

Insured

The valuable employee or owner

Owner and beneficiary

The business in this arrangement

Purpose

Recruitment, continuity and financial loss

06

An executive bonus supports the employee

For a separate retention goal, the company helps a valued executive obtain personally owned life insurance through a bonus arrangement. The executive owns the policy and chooses the beneficiary under its terms. The employer provides the agreed compensation support; that payment alone does not make the employer the policyowner. The arrangement can include tax-related bonus planning, which needs its own review. Keep the core distinction clear: a buy-sell funds an ownership purchase, key-person coverage protects the business, and an executive bonus supports employee-owned protection. Follow the stated objective and the actual contract roles.

Buy-sell

Fund an ownership transfer

Key person

Protect the business’s financial exposure

Executive bonus

Support the employee’s own policy

More detail

The same death can create separate needs

The company may need both money to acquire an owner’s interest and money to cover operating disruption. A single stated amount should not be assumed to fully fund both obligations.

Example: A $600,000 buyout obligation and a separate $150,000 operating need total $750,000 before considering available resources and other assumptions.

Two-owner cross-purchase has two policies in the simple example

Each owner owns one policy on the other. With more owners, a conventional arrangement in which each owner owns a separate policy on every other owner can require n × (n − 1) policies. Other structures are possible, so use the arrangement specified.

Example: Three owners using the stated individual cross-purchase structure require six policies, not three.

A funding shortfall does not rewrite the agreement

If the purchase obligation is $600,000 and available policy proceeds are $500,000, the example has a $100,000 funding gap. The agreement determines the obligation and how any balance is handled.

Example: Do not infer that an underfunded policy automatically reduces the seller’s contractual price.

Consent, interest and tax treatment remain separate

Maryland’s insurable-interest and consent provisions must be considered for business-owned coverage. Corporate employee provisions have specific conditions and protect against the stated retaliation for refusing consent. Federal employer-owned life rules and tax consequences require separate analysis; this role lesson does not promise universal deductibility or tax-free treatment.

Example: The company’s need for funds does not authorize signing an employee’s name or ignoring an applicable consent duty.

A bonus does not automatically become a qualified plan

An executive-bonus life arrangement described here provides compensation support for a policy owned by the executive. It should not be confused with the qualified retirement plans taught later. Details such as bonus amount, taxes and any restrictions need their own agreement and review.

Example: The employer’s payment does not itself make the employer the beneficiary of the employee’s policy.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Follow policy ownership and benefit flows in business arrangements.
  • Distinguish cross-purchase and entity-purchase funding.
  • Match the arrangement to the business’s actual financial problem.
