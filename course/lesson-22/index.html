Explanations & examples

01

One policy can have several labels

Elena compares a personally owned whole life policy with coverage offered through work. Individual and group describe how coverage is arranged. Temporary and permanent describe the intended protection pattern. Participating and nonparticipating describe whether the contract provides for policy dividends. Fixed and variable describe important differences in guarantees and investment risk. These labels answer different questions. One policy can be individual, permanent, participating and fixed at the same time. Ordinary life is a broad individual-life category; do not assume that every use of the word ordinary means one particular premium schedule.

02

Read guarantees and participation carefully

A participating life policy may pay dividends, but those dividends are not guaranteed merely because the policy participates. A fixed design provides the guarantees stated in its contract; the label does not erase charges, funding requirements or exclusions. Variable life uses investment options whose results can affect values and benefits within the policy's terms. The owner bears the specified investment risk. Variable products also involve securities requirements in addition to insurance authority. Before discussing price, know what protection, guarantees, charges and risks are actually being compared. Similar labels do not prove identical contracts.

03

Three foundations of a life premium

The insurer studies mortality: the expected pattern of deaths in the insured group. Interest assumptions reflect earnings on funds held before benefits are paid. Expenses include the costs of acquiring, administering and servicing the business, with appropriate pricing provisions. Holding other assumptions constant, higher expected mortality tends to increase the cost of death protection. Higher assumed interest can reduce the amount that must be collected now for the same future benefit. Greater expense needs increase the gross price. These are pricing relationships, not promises that the premium on an existing policy changes whenever market conditions move.

04

A net single premium isolates the benefit cost

Use a simplified one-year example. Assume a two-in-one-thousand chance of death and a one hundred thousand dollar benefit paid at year end. The expected benefit cost is two hundred dollars per policy before expenses and discounting. If the explicitly assumed interest rate is five percent, dividing two hundred by one point zero five gives a present value of about one hundred ninety dollars and forty-eight cents. This illustrates a net single premium under the stated assumptions. Real life pricing uses fuller mortality and timing data. It is not simply the face amount divided by a guessed lifespan.

05

Annual and gross premiums add other questions

A net single premium is paid once in the simplified model. A net level annual premium spreads funding over the assumed premium-paying period while accounting for timing, interest and the possibility that premiums stop at death. The gross annual premium adds the applicable expense and other pricing provisions. Do not compare a net single amount with a gross annual quote as if they described the same payment. The insurer's actual quoted premium reflects the selected product, risk classification, benefit, guarantees and payment arrangement. A teaching calculation explains a principle; it does not replace an insurer's approved quote.

06

Payment mode changes when money is collected

Elena can pay four hundred eighty dollars annually or forty-two dollars monthly for the stated coverage. Twelve monthly payments total five hundred four dollars, which is twenty-four dollars more per year. Payment mode means frequency, such as annual, semiannual, quarterly or monthly. The monthly amount is not necessarily the annual premium divided by twelve. Compare the total cost as well as affordability and the contract's payment conditions. Keep the final distinctions clear: the product describes the coverage, pricing assumptions help determine its cost, and payment mode describes the schedule on which premiums are collected.

More detail

Product classifications overlap

A group plan can provide temporary life protection; an individually owned policy can be term or permanent. Participation is a separate contract feature. The ordinary-life category covers individual life designs and should not be confused with the specific continuous-pay ordinary whole-life design taught later.

Example: An individual participating whole-life policy carries several compatible classifications.

Variable authority has two sides

Variable life and variable annuity products involve securities regulation as well as insurance regulation. Appropriate state insurance authority and applicable securities registration through the required firm arrangement are separate requirements. Product-specific lessons examine these designs and risks in more detail.

Example: A life-only insurance license by itself does not establish all authority needed to sell a variable product.

Expense loading is not a guaranteed policy dividend

The gross premium includes applicable loading for costs and other pricing provisions. Participating-policy dividends, if declared, arise under the contract and insurer’s experience; their possibility does not make the illustrated amount guaranteed or subtract it automatically from every required premium.

Example: A sales comparison should show the actual premium obligation separately from a possible future dividend.

Check the simple arithmetic and its limits

The one-year illustration is 0.002 × $100,000 = $200 expected year-end benefit, then $200 ÷ 1.05 = $190.476…, rounded to $190.48. It assumes the stated one-year mortality, year-end benefit timing and five-percent interest, and omits expenses. It is not a market rate.

Example: If assumed mortality doubles to 0.004 with the same benefit and interest, the expected benefit cost doubles before any other changes.

Premium timing is part of the calculation

An annual premium stream is valued with the timing and likelihood of future payments in mind. Dividing a net single premium by an arbitrary number of years ignores interest and mortality-related payment cessation.

Example: Twenty expected years of coverage do not by themselves justify dividing every single-premium value by twenty to produce an annual quote.

Mode and premium pattern are different

Monthly is a mode. Level, graded or flexible describes aspects of a premium pattern or product design. A level annual premium can still be paid through the insurer’s available monthly mode at its stated modal cost.

Example: A monthly payment is not proof that the underlying policy is monthly renewable term.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Classify one policy across several independent dimensions.
  • Explain mortality, interest and expense assumptions.
  • Distinguish a net single premium, gross annual premium and payment mode.
