Explanations & examples

01

Start with how long the need lasts

Elena wants a dependable benefit for a lifelong family obligation. Traditional whole life combines permanent death protection with scheduled premiums and contractual cash values. The guarantees depend on meeting the policy's requirements. Compare the guaranteed schedule with any additional illustrated values, and check the sustainable cost. An early surrender may return much less than the premiums paid. The word permanent describes the intended coverage pattern; it does not excuse unpaid required premiums or erase loan obligations. A permanent need can make whole life relevant, but the amount and funding still need to fit the client's circumstances.

02

Continuous pay spreads the required premiums

An ordinary continuous-pay whole life design requires level scheduled premiums over the contract's stated long payment period, commonly through the insured's lifetime or a stated age. The premium can stay level even though the cost associated with aging does not. Early funding and the insurer's reserves support the long-term promise. Do not interpret a level premium as a statement that every dollar is placed in a personal savings account. Review the actual guaranteed cash-value schedule. Also read any stated maturity age and provision instead of assuming every policy ends or pays out at one universal age.

03

Limited pay shortens payments, not protection

Marcus buys a twenty-pay whole life policy at age thirty-five. He makes all twenty required annual payments. The scheduled premium obligation is then complete, while the whole life protection continues under the contract. This is not a twenty-year term policy. A paid-up-at-sixty-five design instead defines the payment period by the stated age. For otherwise comparable coverage, compressing premiums into fewer years generally requires a higher annual payment. Paid up means the required policy premiums have been satisfied. It does not mean the insured has died, the policy has been surrendered, or a death benefit has already been paid.

04

Single premium completes funding at issue

Elena compares another whole life design funded with one substantial premium at issue. A single-premium whole life policy is a form of limited-payment coverage. Its required premium is paid once and it provides the protection and immediate cash values stated in the contract. Paying once does not mean the policy pays the beneficiary once at issue. Nor does it make future loans or withdrawals free of consequences. Large early funding can affect federal modified-endowment-contract treatment. That tax classification requires its own test, which we will study later. Keep the payment design separate from promises about access to money or tax results.

05

Graded premiums follow a changing schedule

A graded-premium whole life design starts with lower scheduled premiums and increases them during an initial period before they become level as the contract states. In a teaching schedule, the annual premium is three hundred dollars in year one, four hundred in year two, and five hundred in year three and afterward. The death benefit stays level in this example. A graded premium is different from a graded death benefit, which limits or changes the benefit during stated early years. Read what is being graded. Do not mistake an attractive first-year price for the continuing premium commitment.

06

Cash value is not an extra automatic death payment

Suppose Marcus's in-force whole life policy states a one-hundred-thousand-dollar death benefit and has twenty thousand dollars of cash value. Assume no loans, added insurance, or other adjustments. The stated death benefit is one hundred thousand dollars, not automatically one hundred twenty thousand. Cash value is a living policy value governed by the contract. Surrender and a death claim are different events. Participating policies may provide dividends, but future dividends are not guaranteed. An illustration showing dividends covering premiums is different from a contractual limited-pay schedule. Read the guaranteed payment obligation before telling someone they can stop paying.

More detail

Paid-up and maturity describe different events

A contract can finish its required premium period well before its stated maturity date. At maturity, apply the actual endowment or extension provision. Read the specified age and continuation terms in the contract.

Example: A 20-pay policy issued at age 35 can complete its premium schedule around age 55 while continuing its protection. That does not make age 55 its maturity age.

The graded example states a level death benefit

Only the premium changes in this teaching schedule. A product with a graded death benefit presents a separate limitation that must be read and disclosed. Product labels do not replace the schedule.

Example: The first three annual premiums total $1,200. The benefit does not become three different amounts simply because the premiums differ.

An illustration is not a paid-up guarantee

Nonguaranteed dividends might offset premiums under an illustrated assumption, but they may be lower. A contractual limited-pay schedule states when the required policy premiums end after timely payment.

Example: If projected dividends fall, a continuous-pay owner may still owe premiums even after the illustration’s projected offset date.

Single premium does not settle tax classification

The funding pattern must be evaluated under the applicable federal tests. Single-premium designs commonly receive modified-endowment-contract treatment, which changes rules for living distributions. The later tax lesson explains the distinction.

Example: Do not call a policy loan tax-free solely because the owner paid the premium once.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Distinguish continuous-pay, limited-pay and single-premium whole life.
  • Separate cash value, death benefit and a paid-up policy.
  • Read a graded premium schedule without confusing it with a graded death benefit.
