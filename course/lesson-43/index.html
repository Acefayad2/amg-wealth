Explanations & examples

01

An added benefit has its own qualifying event

Elena compares four optional features on a life policy. One adds money after a qualifying accidental death. Another preserves opportunities to purchase more protection even if health changes. A third adjusts coverage using an inflation measure. A fourth adds a death benefit linked to premiums under its formula. These features solve different problems. The word rider does not mean every feature pays cash immediately or lasts as long as the base policy. Identify the covered event, the amount, the election conditions and the ending provisions. Then read the cost. A familiar feature name is a starting point, not a substitute for the actual contract.

02

Accidental death does not always mean double

Assume a covered two hundred thousand dollar base death benefit and a fifty thousand dollar accidental-death rider. If the death meets both provisions and no adjustment applies, the total is two hundred fifty thousand dollars. It is not automatically four hundred thousand. Double indemnity describes a design that adds an amount equal to the base benefit; other designs add different amounts. Read whether the accident caused the death, the required time between injury and death, exclusions and the rider's ending age. If a death qualifies under the base policy but not the accident rider, the base benefit may still be payable under its own terms.

03

Guaranteed insurability protects a purchase opportunity

A guaranteed-insurability rider allows specified additional insurance without new evidence of insurability at stated opportunities. Those may be scheduled ages or qualifying events such as marriage, birth or adoption. The actual rider determines which opportunities apply. Suppose Elena's active rider permits a twenty-five thousand dollar increase at the next option date. A health change does not automatically remove that protected opportunity, but she must meet the election requirements and pay the applicable premium. The right is not unlimited insurance whenever she wants it. It also differs from guaranteed renewability, which concerns continuing existing coverage, and conversion, which changes eligible coverage into another permitted form.

04

An inflation rider adjusts the insured amount

A cost-of-living rider can increase life coverage using a stated inflation measure, often the Consumer Price Index. Suppose the question explicitly provides a three percent increase on one hundred thousand dollars of coverage. Three thousand dollars is the increase, so the new amount is one hundred three thousand dollars. This is a coverage calculation, not a three percent cash dividend or guaranteed investment return. Check how often adjustments occur, any limits, the effect of declining an increase and additional insurance costs. Some designs waive new evidence of insurability for permitted increases. Do not assume every rider adjusts annually or provides increased protection at no added cost.

05

Return of premium can describe different promises

A return-of-premium death benefit rider can add an amount related to premiums to the death benefit when the insured dies. Assume the fictional contract provides a two hundred thousand dollar base benefit plus twelve thousand dollars of eligible accumulated premiums, with no deductions or other adjustments. The total is two hundred twelve thousand dollars. Read the formula because withdrawals and charges can affect the rider amount. This differs from return-of-premium term insurance that refunds specified eligible premiums when the insured survives the stated term and meets the policy conditions. Ask whether the triggering event is death or survival. Similar names do not establish identical benefits.

06

Solve the event before choosing the feature

Now Elena asks which feature addresses concern about becoming difficult to insure later. A defined guaranteed-insurability opportunity directly addresses that concern. An accidental-death benefit addresses a different cause-of-death condition. An inflation rider addresses the stated change in coverage. The final rider adds a death benefit based on eligible premiums under its formula. On an exam, underline the event and the requested result. Then calculate only the amounts that the facts trigger. Check whether the option must be elected and whether a deadline, exclusion or coverage limit changes the answer. That approach connects the rider's purpose to the actual case instead of choosing the most attractive sounding feature.

More detail

A rider and its base policy have separate conditions

An accidental-death rider commonly requires a specified causal connection, a death within a stated interval after injury and compliance with exclusions and age limits. Carrier examples have different intervals. Do not replace the exam question’s stated interval with a memorized universal number.

Example: A covered natural-cause death can trigger the base policy while failing the accidental-death rider’s trigger.

Double indemnity identifies a particular benefit relationship

If the additional accidental benefit equals the base death benefit, the combined amount is twice the base. An added $50,000 on a $200,000 base instead totals $250,000 when both qualify and no adjustment applies.

Example: Do not multiply the face amount by two merely because an accident is mentioned.

A purchase right still has conditions

Guaranteed insurability may permit a face-amount increase or new eligible policy at scheduled or event-based opportunities. No new evidence of insurability does not waive the election window, amount limit, permitted product or premium. The cited filing demonstrates these distinctions; its specific ages are not universal Maryland requirements.

Example: A stated $25,000 option is not authority to demand a $500,000 increase at any time.

Inflation adjustments affect coverage and can affect cost

The filed cost-of-living example adjusts on every second anniversary and makes increased coverage subject to additional charges, although the rider itself has no separate charge. Other designs vary. CPI is an inflation measure, not an insurer dividend scale or the policy’s investment return.

Example: A stated 3% adjustment on $100,000 adds $3,000 to coverage.

Distinguish death and survival promises

A premium-linked death rider adds the contract-defined premium amount at death. A return-of-premium term survival feature pays its defined refund when the insured outlives the applicable term and satisfies its conditions. Neither title guarantees interest on every dollar paid or a full refund whenever the policy is surrendered.

Example: Read whether rider charges are included and whether withdrawals reduce the benefit.

Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Calculate a stated accidental-death or premium-linked benefit.
  • Apply a guaranteed-insurability opportunity without confusing it with free coverage.
  • Distinguish an inflation adjustment from a dividend or investment return.
