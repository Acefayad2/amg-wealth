Explanations & examples

01

Roth describes a different tax treatment

Elena considers a Roth individual retirement arrangement. Regular Roth contributions are made with after-tax money and are not deductible. That is different from a traditional contribution that qualifies for a current deduction. Roth eligibility still requires the applicable compensation and contribution rules, and direct regular contributions can be reduced or prohibited by modified adjusted gross income and filing status. A high income that prevents a direct Roth contribution is not the same rule as the traditional deduction phaseout. Nor does it automatically answer whether a conversion is possible. Separate regular contributions, transfers, conversions and distributions before applying a limit. Those words describe different transactions.

02

Traditional and Roth regular contributions share a limit

For twenty twenty-six, a person under age fifty generally has a seven thousand five hundred dollar combined regular contribution limit across traditional and Roth individual retirement arrangements, subject to compensation and other restrictions. It is not a fresh limit for every account. Suppose Elena is forty, has sufficient compensation, qualifies for a full direct Roth contribution and has already contributed three thousand dollars to a traditional account for the year. She generally has forty-five hundred dollars of that combined limit left. The age-fifty catch-up increases the general twenty twenty-six dollar limit by eleven hundred dollars, but does not override a lower compensation amount or Roth income restriction.

03

Regular contributions come out before earnings

When a Roth distribution is not qualified, the ordering rules matter. Ordinary distributions reach regular contributions first, then conversions and applicable rollover amounts under their ordering rules, and earnings last. Aggregate the owner's Roth arrangements as required; opening another account does not restart the order. Suppose Elena has twelve thousand dollars of unrecovered regular Roth contributions and three thousand dollars of earnings, with no conversions, earlier distributions or other adjustments. An ordinary five thousand dollar withdrawal comes from regular contributions and is not subject to income tax or the usual early-distribution additional tax under these facts. Do not treat it as five thousand dollars of earnings merely because the account has grown.

04

A qualified distribution needs the time test and a reason

A qualified Roth distribution can exclude earnings as well as contributions. It generally must occur after the five-tax-year period starting with the first year for which a Roth contribution was made for the owner, and satisfy a qualifying condition. Those conditions include reaching age fifty-nine and one half, qualifying disability, death or an eligible first-home payment within its lifetime limit. Suppose Rosa first contributed for twenty twenty-one and takes a distribution in twenty twenty-six at age sixty-one. The stated time and age conditions are met. Turning sixty-one alone would not satisfy the five-year condition if her first Roth contribution had only just occurred.

05

A conversion has its own additional-tax clock

Converting pretax traditional retirement money to a Roth arrangement generally includes the previously untaxed amount in income for the conversion year. It is not a deduction and does not use the regular contribution limit. A separate five-year additional-tax rule can apply if a younger owner soon withdraws an amount attributable to a taxable conversion. Each conversion has its own clock. For example, an age-forty-five owner converts in twenty twenty-six and withdraws taxable converted amounts in twenty twenty-eight with no applicable exception. An additional tax can apply even though the conversion amount was already included in income earlier. Do not confuse this conversion rule with the five-year qualified-distribution test.

06

Owner rules and beneficiary rules are different

A Roth owner does not have lifetime required minimum distributions from their Roth individual retirement arrangement. That does not mean beneficiaries can always leave inherited funds untouched forever. Death-distribution requirements still depend on beneficiary classification, and the earnings exclusion still requires the applicable qualification rules. Compare the two arrangements carefully. Traditional contributions may provide an eligible deduction now, with later pretax distributions generally taxed. Roth contributions provide no current deduction, but qualified distributions can be excluded. Both require attention to contribution rules and the actual transaction. Neither label by itself establishes a person's best choice. On the exam, identify the tax treatment the stated facts actually support.

More detail

2026 direct Roth contribution income ranges

For 2026, the direct Roth contribution phaseout for single/head-of-household filers is $153,000–$168,000 modified AGI. For joint filers it is $242,000–$252,000. Married-separate filers who lived with a spouse during the year have a $0–$10,000 range; different living-apart facts matter. These are contribution restrictions, not the traditional deduction ranges.

Example: Eligibility to convert is a separate question from eligibility for a regular direct contribution.

Order and aggregation prevent selective labeling

Nonqualified Roth IRA distributions generally use regular contributions first; then conversions and applicable qualified-plan rollovers, oldest first, with the taxable portion before the nontaxable portion of each; then earnings. Roth-to-Roth rollovers are treated under the separate aggregation rules. Corrective excess-contribution distributions have special treatment.

Example: The $5,000 example assumes $12,000 unrecovered regular contributions and no conversions or adjustments.

Five tax years is not five anniversaries of a deposit

For a calendar-year owner whose first Roth contribution is for 2021, the general qualified-distribution period runs from January 1, 2021 through December 31, 2025. A qualifying age or other condition must also be met. A 2026 conversion’s separate five-year early-access clock starts January 1, 2026.

Example: First-home qualification has a $10,000 lifetime limit and additional conditions; it is not an unlimited earnings exclusion.

Conversion is not a second taxation of basis

A conversion generally includes the pretax portion in income, with the required traditional IRA basis allocation when after-tax amounts exist. Later early withdrawal of taxable converted amounts can trigger the separate 10% recapture rule within the applicable five-year period, unless an exception applies. The converted principal is not simply included in ordinary income a second time.

Example: Reaching 59½ can remove the additional-tax issue without replacing the separate qualification test for earnings.

Death does not waive every Roth requirement

The owner’s lifetime RMD exemption does not eliminate beneficiary distribution requirements. If the original qualification period has not been met, a beneficiary’s earnings distribution can still be taxable even though the death exception removes the usual early-distribution additional tax. A spouse’s options and other beneficiary categories require separate review.

Example: Current designated Roth employer accounts also have no owner lifetime RMD requirement; do not memorize an outdated opposite rule.

Learning objectives

  • Apply the ordering rule to a simple withdrawal.
  • Distinguish qualified distributions from conversion additional-tax rules.
